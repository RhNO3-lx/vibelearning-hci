Abstract
The rapid advancement of large language mod-
els (LLMs) has driven the deployment of LLM-
based AI tutors on online learning platforms.
This widespread adoption highlights an urgent
need for systematic benchmarks to evaluate
their tutoring capabilities. However, existing
evaluations predominantly focus on isolated,
short-term interactions, overlooking the inher-
ently long-term nature of learning. To bridge
this gap, we introduce LongTutor, a benchmark
for long-term personalized tutoring grounded in
formative assessment theory. Built from expert-
annotated real-world learning logs, LongTu-
tor evaluates LLMs across three progressive
tasks: historical evidence acquisition, knowl-
edge state diagnosis, and adaptive teaching
action. Our experiments reveal a critical ca-
pability mismatch: while LLMs excel at evi-
dence acquisition, they struggle to effectively
leverage long-term history for accurate diagno-
sis and adaptive teaching. To enable scalable
benchmark expansion, we further propose an
automated generator–verifier pipeline, paving
the way toward truly long-term AI tutoring
systems. We release our code and dataset at
https://github.com/liano3/LongTutor.

dents when they provide incorrect answers. Along-

records. According to formative assessment the-
ory (Black and Wiliam, 1998), effective tutoring
should leverage students’ long-term learning his-
tory and follow an Evidence–Diagnosis–Teaching
paradigm (VanLehn, 2006). In this work, we
focus on structured, verifiable subjects such as
mathematics, where these latent states and down-
stream teaching outcomes can be annotated with
high reliability. As illustrated in Figure 1, rather
than providing generic, context-isolated hints for
a "Stair Climbing" problem, the tutor should first
elicit evidence (a prior successful experience on
the structurally analogous "Wood Cutting" prob-
lem, 11 days prior) from the student’s long-term
learning history. Based on this evidence, the tu-
tor diagnoses the student’s current knowledge state
as Transfer Deficit, which means failure to recog-
nize the shared structure between the two problems.
Guided by this diagnosis, the tutor adapts its teach-
ing by linking current difficulties to prior experi-
ences, thereby scaffolding new understanding onto
existing knowledge.
Aligned with the Evidence–Diagnosis–Teaching
paradigm, we introduce LongTutor, the first bench-
mark dedicated to long-term personalized tutoring.
It contains three progressive tasks: (1) Evidence
Acquisition tests the tutor’s fundamental ability to
acquire related evidences from long-term history
as the basis for diagnosis, including information
extraction (e.g., question text, knowledge points)
from single records, multi-session reasoning across
multiple interactions (Wei et al., 2022) (e.g., num-
ber of questions done in a specific day), and hallu-
cination checks to prevent fabrication (Zhang et al.,
2025a) (e.g., trap questions with false premises).
(2) State Diagnosis evaluates the tutor’s analyt-
ical ability to perform cognitive inference, root-
ing student’s current error to specific knowledge
states (e.g., conceptual gap), which aligned with
the Knowledge Dimensions of the Revised Bloom’s
Taxonomy (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001). (3)
Teaching Actionassesses the tutor’s ability to scaf-
fold the problem-solving process using adaptive
teaching strategies (e.g., analogical transfer (Gick
and Holyoak, 1980)) that are derived from the ac-
quired evidence and diagnosed knowledge states.
Conducting effective evaluation on these tasks
requires long-term student learning trajectories
paired with high-quality diagnostic and instruc-
tional annotations. However, such data are ex-
tremely scarce. While existing datasets like
MoocRadar (Yu et al., 2023) and XES3G5M (Liu
et al., 2023b) contain long-term histories (i.e., se-
quences of problem-solving records containing
problem texts and timestamps), they lack the fine-
grained expert labels necessary to assess state diag-
nosis and teaching actions.
To address the data scarcity challenge, we
enriched real-world problem-solving logs (from
XES3G5M (Liu et al., 2023b)) through human
expert annotation. To ensure the reliability of
the ground truth, we adhered to a rigorous qual-
ity control protocol involving independent dual-
expert labeling followed by adjudication. This pro-
cess yielded a high inter-annotator agreement (with
Cohen’s Kappa = 0.90), confirming the trustwor-
thiness of our labels. Consequently, this expert-
annotated dataset serves as the core test set within
our benchmark.
Using this benchmark to evaluate mainstream
LLMs, we observe a distinct performance gap:
while models demonstrate proficiency in basic evi-
dence acquisition, they struggle significantly with
the more complex tasks of state diagnosis and
teaching action. This reveals that current LLMs
fail to effectively leverage students’ learning histo-
ries for long-term personalized tutoring, indicating
substantial room for improvement. Crucially, these
findings underscore the significance of our bench-
mark in advancing personalized education.
Moreover, as the annotation process is labor-
intensive and time-consuming, we propose an auto-
mated generator–verifier pipeline to support scal-
able expansion across larger datasets and multiple
subject domains. The pipeline synthesizes high-
quality data from raw logs and demonstrates strong
label and ranking consistency against human anno-
tations (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.81), providing a reli-
able and low-cost path for benchmark expansion.

2.2 Long-Term Interaction Benchmarks
Beyond the educational domain, evaluating LLMs
in long-term scenarios has become a critical
research direction. In open-domain dialogue,
benchmarks like MSC (Multi-Session Chat) (Xu
et al., 2022) evaluate the model’s ability to
maintain persona consistency across disparate
sessions. Similarly, long-context benchmarks
like LOCOMO (Maharana et al., 2024), In-
finiteBench (Zhang et al., 2024) and Long-
MemEval (Wu et al., 2025) test the model’s ca-
pacity to process and reason over extended histor-
ical inputs, often utilizing "needle-in-a-haystack"
retrieval tasks (Liu et al., 2024c).
Despite these advancements, a significant mis-
alignment exists between these open-domain
benchmarks and the requirements of educational
tutoring. These benchmarks typically focus on in-
formation retention (e.g., recalling a user’s past
statement), but tutoring requires inferring latent
knowledge states and take teaching action.
3 LongTutor Benchmark

term personalized tutoring and detail the construc-
tion of LongTutor. As illustrated in Figure 2, our
benchmark structures the evaluation around three

and teaching action.

Task 1: Evidence Acquisition. The prerequi-
site for personalized tutoring is a robust under-
standing of the student’s long-term history. To
ensure subsequent diagnoses are grounded in fact
rather than hallucination, this task evaluates the
ability to acquire historical information across three
dimensions: (1) Information Extraction targets
specific details within a single record, (2) Multi-
session Reasoning requires aggregating patterns
across multiple interactions, and (3) Hallucination
Check tests the robustness against false premises.
As shown in Figure 2, the model must extract
the problem text from Q[136], count the "paral-
lelogram" questions on 2025-10-11 by combining
records Q[152] and Q[136], and correctly output
"Unknown" for Q[114] when queried with a mis-
matched topic (parallelograms vs. triangles). These
probes operationalize trace-level grounding under
information overload, rather than serving as stan-
dalone IR exercises.
Task 2: State Diagnosis. Once the evidence is
acquired, the model diagnoses the student’s latent
knowledge state to identify why the error occurred
to support adaptive teaching action. We formu-
late this as a multi-class classification task. To
ensure pedagogical rigor, we ground our diagnostic
framework in the Revised Bloom’s Taxonomy (An-
derson and Krathwohl, 2001), which categorizes
knowledge into four dimensions: Factual, Concep-
tual, Procedural, and Metacognitive. We map these
dimensions to a specific label set:Y = {Recall Fail-
ure, Conceptual Gap, Procedural Error, Transfer
Deficit}. This task requires the model to predict
the correct diagnosis labelydiag∈Ybased on his-
torical evidence, thereby dictating the subsequent
teaching action. Detailed definitions and symp-
tomatic descriptions of this taxonomy are provided
in Appendix D.
Task 3: Teaching Action. Finally, the ultimate
objective is to translate the diagnostic insight into
an effective teaching action. This task evaluates
whether the model can generate a teaching response
Rint explicitly leveraging the student’s long-term
learning history and executing history-aware teach-
ing strategies. For instance, as shown in Figure 2,
the model observes that the student has a Recall
Failure on a basic parallelogram question after
an 11-day interval of successful practice on com-
plex ones. The generated response directly refer-
ences the student’s past success on a complex par-
allelogram problem (Q[136]) from 11 days prior,

3.2 Evaluation Protocols
We propose a multi-dimensional evaluation proto-
col suite corresponding to the three subtasks. De-
tailed definitions and calculation formulas are pro-
vided in Appendix G.
Evidence Acquisition. We evaluate retrieval pre-
cision using Semantic Accuracy (SA). This metric
extends standard Exact Match (EM) by incorporat-
ing an LLM-based semantic equivalence check to
account for correct answers that are paraphrased.
State Diagnosis. We treat state diagnosis as a
multi-class classification task. To address potential
class imbalances among cognitive states, we pri-
marily report the Macro-F1 Score. We also provide
Accuracy for a comprehensive performance view.
Teaching Action. Due to the open-ended nature
of teaching, we adopt a dual-view evaluation. We
report ROUGE-L (Lin, 2004) only as a supple-
mentary lexical-overlap reference, since pedagogi-
cally strong responses need not match the reference
wording. Our primary assessment uses an LLM-
as-a-Judge framework (Liu et al., 2023a; Zheng
et al., 2023) with an instance-specific pedagogi-
cal rubric. Specifically, the judge evaluates each
response based on four dimensions: (i) History Uti-
lization measures how effectively the model lever-
ages the student’s learning history; (ii) Strategy
Alignment assesses whether the feedback adheres
to the diagnosed teaching strategy (Shute, 2008);
(iii) Coherence evaluates the logical structure and
linguistic clarity of the response; and (iv) Appro-
priateness examines whether the difficulty are ap-
propriate (Vygotsky, 1978).
3.3 Data Annotation
To construct reliable evaluation for long-term per-
sonalized tutoring, we curate a human-annotated
dataset with ground-truth labels for the three tasks.
Data Preparation. The proposed tasks necessi-
tate detailed problem texts for semantic evidence
acquisition and timestamps for tracking tempo-
ral dynamics (e.g., forgetting behaviors). Con-
sequently, we build upon XES3G5M (Liu et al.,
2023b), a dataset of real-world primary school
mathematics problem-solving sequences. To en-
sure long-term history, we filter for sequences with
lengthL> 200 and a span exceeding 7 days. This
yields 3,437 structured histories, from which 1,000
samples were randomly selected for manual anno-
tation as our gold-standard test set.
Annotation Protocol. To ensure the highest stan-
dard of pedagogical validity, we recruited three
highly qualified domain experts, all holding ad-
vanced degrees in Education and possessing pro-
found theoretical knowledge alongside practical
teaching experience. Following established anno-
tation practices (Klie et al., 2024; Macina et al.,
2023), we employ a rigorous protocol involving in-
dependent dual expert annotation followed by adju-
dication to ensure label reliability. Specifically, two
experts independently labeled each sample to pro-
duce evidence Q&A, diagnostic labels, and teach-
ing actions. Any discrepancies were subsequently
reviewed and resolved by the third senior expert
to establish the final gold standard. Please refer to
Appendix E for the detailed procedures.
Quality Validation. To quantitatively assess la-
bel reliability, we computed Cohen’s Kappa ( κ)
between the two primary experts over the entire
dataset. The resulting agreement score for diagnos-
tic labels wasκ= 0.90, indicating an exceptional
level of consistency. The third senior expert was
involved solely for adjudication, ensuring that the
final ground truth strictly aligns with pedagogical
standards. Illustrative samples covering all four
diagnostic categories are provided in Appendix F.

4.2 Main Results
Table 2 presents the comparative results on the
LongTutor benchmark. Aligned with our three
evaluation dimensions, we highlight the follow-
ing key findings. For supplementary analysis (e.g.,
instruction following issues) and performance met-
rics of additional open-source models, please refer
to Appendix I and Table 7.
Evidence Acquisition: The Reasoning Gap.
While models generally exhibit proficiency in
surface-level retrieval, they struggle significantly
with reasoning over long contexts. As shown in Ta-
ble 2, top-tier models like Gemini-2.5-Pro achieve
near-perfect scores in information extraction (IE:
97.0%), yet their performance drops precipitously
in multi-session reasoning (MR: 62.8%). This gap
is even more pronounced in open-source models
(e.g., Llama-3.1 drops from 51.6% to 12.2%), re-
vealing that current LLMs rely heavily on local
pattern matching rather than synthesizing global in-
formation. Furthermore, the varying performance
on hallucination check (HC) indicates that distinct
memory boundaries remain a challenge, with mod-
els often fabricating details when evidence is ab-
sent.
State Diagnosis: A Universal Challenge. The
task of inferring latent knowledge states proves to
be exceptionally difficult. We observe a universal
underperformance across all models, with even the
strongest closed-source model (Gemini-2.5-Pro)
achieving a Macro-F1 of only 39.7%. Most open-
source models struggle to surpass 20% F1. This
low baseline underscores the high research value of
the state diagnosis task: unlike standard QA, cor-
rectly identifying specific knowledge states (e.g.,

differentiating a conceptual gap from a procedural
error) requires a depth of cognitive modeling that
current general-purpose LLMs have yet to master.
Teaching Action: The History Deficit and Spe-
cialization Tax. In the final teaching action stage,
most models fail to effectively leverage histori-
cal evidence to personalize teaching. The His-
tory Utilization (Hist.) scores are consistently
low (mostly < 2.0 on a 5-point scale), indicat-
ing that generated feedback is often generic rather
than tailored to the student’s long-term learning
history. Notably, we observe a "Specialization
Tax": education-specific models (e.g., EduChat-7B,
EDU-Qwen-2.5-7B) consistently lag behind their
base model (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B) in all tasks. For
instance, EDU-Qwen2.5-7B scores significantly
lower in evidence acquisition (28.9 vs. 37.3) and
teaching Action (1.8 vs. 2.2) compared to its base
model. This suggests that current domain-specific
fine-tuning (e.g., short-form or instruction-dense
datasets) may inadvertently erode the models’ in-
herent long-context robustness.
Diagnosis Failure Modes: A Fine-grained View.
Beyond the overall F1 scores, we identify three
distinct failure patterns that hinder effective state
diagnosis: Category Collapse, where models (e.g.,
GLM-4.6) completely fail to identify specific
knowledge states like Recall Failure; Diagnostic
Hallucination, characterized by aggressive over-
prediction of complex states like Transfer Deficits;
and a pervasive Majority Class Bias. These pat-
terns suggest that current models lack the nuance
required for high-stakes pedagogical reasoning. A
comprehensive breakdown of these model-specific
issues is provided in Appendix I.2.
Qualitative Analysis. Appendix I.3 presents a
case comparison between GPT-5 and GPT-5-mini,
illustrating how identifying a Transfer Deficit en-
ables significantly lower cognitive load for the stu-
dent.

6 Conclusion
We presented LongTutor, the first benchmark eval-
uating LLMs on long-term personalized tutoring
via an Evidence–Diagnosis–Teaching framework.
Unlike existing single-session evaluations, Long-
Tutor assesses LLMs on utilizing learning histo-
ries for personalized tutoring. Our experiments
reveal a significant gap: while LLMs excel at his-
torical evidence acquisition, they struggle to trans-
late evidence into accurate diagnoses and adaptive
teaching, indicating that retrieval capability alone
is insufficient. Additionally, we validated a gener-
ator–verifier pipeline to address data scarcity. We
hope LongTutor fosters the development of AI tu-
tors that can harness the full potential of long-term
learning history to provide truly personalized and
pedagogically intelligent guidance.
Limitations
While LongTutor provides a robust framework for
long-term personalized tutoring evaluation, several
limitations remain. First, our current focus is pri-
marily on mathematics, where errors follow clear
logical patterns; the applicability of our framework
to subjective disciplines like creative writing re-
mains to be explored. Second, despite our recip-
rocal verification mechanism, the synthetic data
generated by frontier models may still harbor sub-
tle inductive biases or specific pedagogical pref-
erences that differ from diverse human teaching
styles. Finally, our benchmark evaluates static his-
torical snapshots rather than dynamic, real-time in-
teractions, and the long-term scope is currently con-
strained by model context windows. Future work
will aim to address these issues by incorporating
interactive simulators and more efficient memory
architectures for extensive student histories.

E Annotation Protocol Details
To ensure the high quality and consistency of the
LongTutor benchmark, we implemented a struc-
tured three-step annotation pipeline. As mentioned
in the main text, all steps were conducted under
independent dual expert annotation followed by ad-
judication to ensure label reliability. The specific
procedures for each task are as follows:
Step 1: Evidence Query Construction. Annota-
tors designed probing queries to rigorously evaluate
history retrieval and reasoning capabilities. These
queries were constructed to cover three distinct cat-
egories: (1) specific record queries targeting factual
details from a single interaction record; (2) cross-
session reasoning queries requiring the aggregation
of information or identification of patterns across
multiple historical sessions; and (3) hallucination
traps asking about non-existent records or absent
details, which require the model to correctly ab-
stain rather than fabricating information.
Step 2: Diagnostic State Labeling. Given the
student’s current incorrectly answered problem and
the full learning historyHt, annotators inferred the
underlying cognitive cause of the error. They as-
signed a diagnostic label based on our predefined

Models. We utilized a combination of local de-
ployment and API access to evaluate the wide range
of models: (1) Open-Source Models: Sourced from
Hugging Face4. Models fitting within local mem-
ory were deployed using the vLLM library (Kwon
et al., 2023). (2) Large-Scale Models: Models
exceeding local memory limits (e.g., GLM-4.6)
were accessed via third-party cloud APIs, includ-
ing OpenRouter 5 and SiliconFlow 6. (3) Propri-
etary Models: Evaluated directly via their official
provider APIs.
Hardware Configuration. Local experiments
were conducted on 2 ×NVIDIA A100 (40GB)
GPUs, and a 16-core Intel Xeon Gold 6426Y CPU.
Inference Hyperparameters. To ensure repro-
ducibility and deterministic outputs, we applied a
uniform decoding strategy across all models: (1)
Temperature: Set to T = 0.0 to minimize ran-
domness (greedy decoding). (2) Generation Limit:
Maximum generation length set to 3,000 tokens to
accommodate long teaching responses. (3) Judge
Configuration: For the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation
in Task 3, we utilized Gemini-3-Flash withT = 0.0
to ensure consistent scoring standards. Evaluation
is performed on each sample individually, and the
final results are reported as the average across the
entire dataset.
Input & Prompting Strategy. To simulate a re-
alistic long-term tutoring scenario, we adhere to
a standardized input protocol for all tasks: (1)
Context Window (Ht): We construct a long-term
context consisting of the student’s most recent
L = 100 practice records. Each historical record
explicitly includes the question text, associated
knowledge points, interaction timestamp, and the
correctness label (Correct/Incorrect). (2) Target
Problem: The sequence concludes with the current
incorrectly answered problem, which serves as the
target for diagnosis and teaching. (3) Prompt For-
mat: We employ a unified zero-shot prompt tem-

I.3 Qualitative Case Study
To demonstrate the concrete impact of model ca-
pability on utilizing long-term student history, we
present a detailed comparison in Table 14.
Scenario Overview. The student failed to solve
a new path-counting problem (Q8285) that intro-
duced a specific constraint ("move from smaller
to larger numbers"). Crucially, the input history
included successful attempts at "Shortest Path La-
beling" problems (e.g., Q779–Q784), indicating
the student possesses the underlying skill but failed
to adapt it.
Analysis of Model Behaviors. The performance
of different models illustrates a clear divide in
how historical data is utilized to guide pedagog-
ical decisions. Model A (GPT-5-mini), despite
having access to the student history, failed to lever-
age specific prior knowledge and defaulted to a
generic "Procedural Error" diagnosis. This led to
a high-cognitive-load teaching involving techni-
cal concepts like "Directed Graphs" and "Dynamic
Programming," which likely exceeds the student’s
zone of proximal development and ignores their
actual learning trajectory. In contrast, Model B
(GPT-5) accurately identified a "Transfer Deficit"
by recognizing that the student possesses the un-
derlying skill ("Labeling Method") but failed to
adapt it to the new context. Consequently, Model
B generated an Analogical Transfer cue that ex-
plicitly linked the current problem to the student’s
past successes. This approach effectively facil-
itates knowledge transfer and reduces cognitive
load, showcasing a more sophisticated level of true
personalization.
