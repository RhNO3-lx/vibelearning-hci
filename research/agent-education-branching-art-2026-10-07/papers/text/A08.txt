Negotiating creator identity: agency and ethical awareness 
in AI‑assisted art education
Hyejeong Lee1  · Wonjin Yu2 · Juwon Lee3
Received: 16 May 2026 / Accepted: 15 June 2026 
© The Author(s) 2026, modified publication 2026
Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is influencing creative work, negotiating not only how artists create but also how they understand 
their own identity as creators. This study examines how art high school students navigate AI’s role in their creative processes, 
using identity formation theory as a framework. While existing research primarily focuses on AI’s technical benefits, such 
as efficiency and productivity, there has been little exploration of how AI alters young creators’ self-concept and autonomy. 
This study investigates whether students perceive AI as a mere tool, a collaborator, or a potential threat to their creative 
identity. It also explores how AI’s increasing presence affects students’ confidence in their originality, their sense of author-
ship, and their ethical concerns about AI-generated content, particularly regarding copyright and creative ownership. The 
findings reveal that students have mixed views on AI: some embrace it as a source of inspiration and efficiency, while others 
express discomfort about its potential to undermine human creativity. These results highlight the need for AI education that 
extends beyond technical training to include discussions on ethics, authorship, and career adaptability in an AI-driven crea-
tive industry. By understanding how students construct their creative identities in relation to AI, educators and policymakers 
can better support them in navigating an evolving digital landscape.
 Keywords artificial intelligence · art education ·  creator identity ·  identity formation · ethical awareness
1 Introduction
The rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) has trans-
formed a wide range of domains, including education and 
the creative industries. In creative contexts, AI presents a 
qualitatively different challenge from traditional digital tech-
nologies. Rather than simply supporting task performance, 
generative AI actively participates in idea generation and 
content creation, thereby influencing the nature of creative 
work itself (Millet et al. 2023; Shin 2021).
This shift complicates the process of technology adop-
tion. As AI becomes embedded in creative workflows, users 
may no longer perceive it as a passive tool, but as an active 
agent that influences both process and outcome (Jiang et al. 
2024; Lee and Park, 2024). In artistic domains, this transfor-
mation raises fundamental questions about authorship, origi-
nality, and the role of human (Shin 2026b). The growing 
presence of generative AI in creative industries has intensi-
fied tensions between automation and artistic authorship. 
While AI offers practical benefits such as efficiency, speed, 
and inspiration, it also introduces concerns regarding owner-
ship, fairness, and the legitimacy of AI-assisted work. These 
tensions are particularly salient for adolescents, who are in 
the process of forming their identities as creators and future 
professionals.
Despite the rapid integration of AI into creative practices, 
existing research has largely focused on technical skills, tool 
use, and efficiency-oriented outcomes. Relatively little atten-
tion has been paid to how young learners interpret AI’s role 
in creative processes, or how these experiences shape their

2  Literature review
2.1  AI acceptance of AI technologies
When new technologies emerge and develop, user accept-
ance of those technologies has become an important topic. 
One of the most widely used theoretical frameworks in these 
discussions is the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) 
(Davis 1989; Choung et al. 2023; Kelly et al. 2023 ). The 
TAM explains that technology acceptance is primarily influ-
enced by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use 
(Choung et al. 2023; Shin 2021). In other words, the more 
users perceive a technology as improving efficiency and pro-
ductivity, the higher the likelihood they will accept it (Ger -
lich 2023; Sohn and Kwon 2020). Trust in technology is also 
discussed as a crucial factor influencing the acceptance of AI 
(Mcknight et al. 2011). This trust is formed based on percep-
tions regarding the reliability, transparency, and fairness of 
the system (Rane et al. 2024). These technology acceptance 
factors also apply to AI, particularly in how users understand 
and trust the decision-making processes and outcomes of AI 
systems (Rane et al. 2024). Based on this perspective, exist-
ing studies have primarily relied on the TAM framework to 
explain user acceptance of technology (Jiang et al. 2024; 
Kelly et al. 2023). In particular, many studies related to AI 
acceptance tend to focus on how to facilitate user accept-
ance (Rane et al. 2024). This approach presupposes AI as a 
functional tool, and discussion on how users understand and 
interpret the role of AI is relatively limited. These studies 
also do not always present consistent results regarding AI 
acceptance (Jiang et al. 2024).
AI is developing at a much faster pace than existing tech-
nologies and is demonstrating a new technological paradigm 
that actively intervenes in task performance, moving beyond 
technology used merely passively. These changes increase 
the likelihood that AI will be perceived not merely as an 
execution tool, but as an entity that intervenes in human 
judgment and creative processes (Millet et al. 2023). Due 
to these characteristics, it is argued that understanding AI 
acceptance requires discussion within a broader context that 
extends beyond TAM (Jiang et al. 2024; Kelly et al. 2023). 
For example, while some studies report high levels of user 
acceptance of AI technology, others find significant levels 
of resistance (Jiang et al. 2024). These conflicting results 
may be due to the fact that attitudes toward AI can vary 
depending on how users perceive the role of AI and what 
the context is.
Jiang et al. (2024) suggest that attitudes toward AI accept-
ance may differ depending on whether users perceive AI as 
a service provider that assists human activities or as a task 
replacement system. As such, the perception of the role of 
AI goes beyond mere acceptance and is also connected to 
how users define their own roles. In fact, AI systems that 
support human activities, such as shopping recommen-
dations or customer service, and AI systems that directly 
replace human labor can elicit different user reactions. Fur-
thermore, studies have reported that user backlash may occur 
if AI is perceived as a threat to uniquely human cognitive 
abilities, such as artistic creativity (Longoni et al. 2019; 
Millet et al. 2023). This reaction is not merely a rejection 
of technology but is related to the perception that unique 
human creativity or expertise is under threat, and it can be 
particularly strong in areas closely related to identity, such as 
creative work. (Millet et al. 2023; Strich et al. 2021). These 
reactions may be related to perceptions of threats to profes-
sional identity or technological overload, and these factors 
can act as deterrents to AI-based systems (Kim et al. 2022; 
Strich et al. 2021).
Overall, user attitudes and perceptions regarding AI 
acceptance may be influenced by various contextual factors 
rather than exhibiting consistent patterns (Jiang et al. 2024). 
AI acceptance, therefore, needs to be understood not merely 
as the usefulness or ease of use of the technology, but as a 
process of interpreting how users perceive AI and how it

being explained solely by factors such as technical char -

2.2  Human–AI agency in learning environments
Human agency refers to an individual’s ability to act inde-
pendently and influence their environment (Bandura 2001; 
Mouta et al. 2025). Humans are described as autonomous 
beings who shape their own behavior through abilities such 
as intentionality, self-regulation, prediction, and self-reflec-
tion (Bandura 2006). This agency is understood not merely 
as a characteristic of the individual, but as a process formed 
within social relationships and temporal contexts (Bandura 
2006). This perspective is also significantly applicable in the 
context of education. In other words, learners can be under-
stood not merely as recipients of knowledge, but as agents 
who actively participate in their own learning process.
Beyond questions of acceptance, the growing involve-
ment of AI in decision-making and content generation raises 
important questions about agency. This is particularly sig-
nificant because agency has traditionally been understood 
as a uniquely human capacity (Schreiber, 2024). As AI has 
moved beyond being a passive tool and begun to actively 
intervene in human activities through content creation, 
decision support, and problem-solving, this assumption is 
increasingly challenged (Krakowski 2025). Due to these 
changes, some studies understand AI technology from the 
perspective of human-AI interaction (e.g., Courtois and Tim-
mermans 2018) or propose the concept of machine agency 
(e.g., Pickering et al. 2017; Sundar 2020). In particular, since 
human and machine agencies coexist in AI-based environ-
ments, tensions regarding the distribution of authority and 
control may arise between humans and AI (Jia et al. 2012; 
Kang and Lou 2022; Kang and Kim 2020). For example, 
while users may positively evaluate the efficiency and con-
venience provided by AI, they may simultaneously experi-
ence concerns that human control may be weakened (Jia 
et al. 2012; Kang and Kim 2020).
This tension extends beyond simple functional interaction 
between humans and AI to the question of ‘who makes the 
decision’ and ‘who takes responsibility’ regarding judgments 
when using AI (Jia et al. 2012; Kang and Kim 2020). In other 
words, learners may perceive AI as a simple tool or as an 
entity that influences decision-making, and this difference 
in perception directly affects the agency experience. Further-
more, human-AI interaction can manifest in various forms, 
such as cooperation, control, and resistance. Representative 
concepts explaining these relationships are automation and 
augmentation (Krakowski 2025; Raisch and Krakowski 
2021). Automation refers to AI replacing tasks previously 
performed by humans, while augmentation refers to humans 
and AI collaborating to expand upon those tasks (Raisch and 
Krakowski 2021). This perspective suggests the potential for 
AI technology to evolve into a hybrid agency where humans 
and AI work together to solve problems, rather than com-
pletely replacing humans (Raisch and Krakowski 2021). This 
distinction between automation and augmentation is closely 
linked to how learners perceive AI and define their own roles 
(Raisch and Krakowski 2021). For instance, if AI is perceived 
as an auxiliary tool, agency is maintained; however, if AI 
is perceived as driving the outcome, it can lead to a loss of 
control (Jia et al. 2012; Kang and Kim 2020).
This discussion holds significant implications in the con-
text of education. This is because, as AI technology actively 
intervenes in learning support, content creation, and prob-
lem-solving processes, the learner’s role and mode of control 
may also change. Consequently, in educational research, it 
is becoming increasingly important to understand learners 
as active agents and to adopt a perspective that considers 
their agency (Mouta et al. 2025). This perspective is par -
ticularly important for adolescent learners, as they are in 
a critical period for identity formation where individuals 
explore their abilities and roles (Erikson 1988). Adolescent 
learners form diverse identity experiences while exploring 
their capabilities and potential through new technologies and 
creative activities (Kay 2018). However, there has not yet 
been sufficient discussion on how learners experience and 
interpret their agency in an AI-based environment.
Furthermore, AI technology exhibits characteristics dis-
tinct from existing technologies. For instance, unlike tradi-
tional technologies where users select and execute specific 
functions (e.g., coloring), generative AI demonstrates a top-
down interaction style that automatically generates results 
beyond user input (Mouta et al. 2025). This top-down inter -
action alters the structure of interaction between users and 
technology, raising concerns that it may weaken learners’ 
agency. Changes in this interaction structure can have a 
direct impact on the sense of control and agency that learn-
ers experience in their creative process (Mouta et al. 2025). 
Therefore, in the context of AI education, an approach that 
considers learners’ agency and participation experiences 
alongside the functionality of the technology itself is neces-
sary (Mouta et al. 2025).
2.3  Creator identity formation
Identity formation is described as a developmental process 
in which an individual seeks to understand who they are 
and how they relate to society (Erikson 1988; Hamman 
and Hendricks 2005). Erikson’s psychosocial development

theory explains human development as a lifelong process of 
identity formation (Erikson 1968; Hamman and Hendricks 
2005). Individuals form their values and roles through vari-
ous social experiences and role exploration. This process 
of identity formation takes place not only through internal 
exploration but also through interaction with social and cul-
tural contexts.
Particularly, adolescence is understood as a crucial stage 
in identity development, serving as a period for explor -
ing one’s future roles, values, and social position (Erikson 
1988). During this period, individuals experiment with 
multiple possibilities and re-examine existing beliefs and 
norms. Erikson (1988) explained that adolescents form their 
identity through evaluating values and norms conveyed by 
social environments such as family, school, and media. 
Additionally, Blos (1967) described adolescence as the “sec-
ond individuation stage,” emphasizing the emergence of an 
independent identity distinct from family. Because identity 
formation occurs through continuous interaction with social 
and environmental contexts, technological environments can 
also serve as important spaces where identity is constructed 
and negotiated. Digital technologies provide adolescents 
with expanded opportunities to access information, engage 
in social interaction, and experiment with different forms 
of self-expression (Katz and Aakhus 2002). At the same 
time, these environments may also introduce challenges 
such as information overload, fragmented self-presentation, 
and identity confusion (Israelashvili et al. 2012; Misra and 
Stokols 2012).
In identity formation theory, creative activity plays a par-
ticularly important role, as it allows individuals to express 
their thoughts, values, and abilities while constructing 
a sense of self through meaningful production (Hamman 
and Hendricks 2005). For adolescents, engaging in creative 
activities provides opportunities to explore their capabilities, 
communicate socially, and negotiate their identities within 
broader cultural contexts. In this context, AI differs from 
conventional technologies in its capacity to more actively 
intervene in the identity formation process through interac-
tion and co-generation. Specifically, generative AI can influ-
ence how individuals perceive their roles and contributions 
by directly participating in content creation and decision-
making processes (Schreiber, 2024).
However, in AI-based creative environments, the struc-
ture of creative processes is fundamentally transformed. AI 
can intervene in multiple stages of creation, including idea 
generation, image production, and writing, thereby blurring 
the boundaries of authorship and contribution (Schreiber, 
2024). As a result, learners must continuously interpret and 
renegotiate their roles in relation to AI during the creative 
process. Through these interactions, learners may recon-
struct their identities not merely as technology users, but 
as creators. By comparing and interpreting AI-generated 
outputs alongside their own contributions, learners engage 
with the question of “who is the creator”, leading to a redefi-
nition of their roles. This process reflects a core mechanism 
of identity formation, as identity is shaped through ongoing 
interaction with social and technological contexts (Erikson 
1988; Katz and Aakhus 2002).
Importantly, learners’ perceptions of AI’s role influence 
this process in different ways. When AI is perceived as 
dominant in the creative process, learners may experience a 
diminished sense of control and perceive their creator iden-
tity as threatened (Kang and Lou 2022; Jiang et al. 2024). In 
contrast, when AI is understood as a supportive tool, learn-
ers are more likely to maintain agency and strengthen their 
sense of identity through perspectives of augmentation and 
human–AI collaboration (Raisch and Krakowski 2021).
Despite these emerging insights, research examining 
how learners’ creator identity develops and transforms in 
AI-based creative environments, and how these identity 
processes relate to their perceptions of and reactions to AI 
remains limited.
2.4  Ethical and authorship challenges 
in AI‑generated content
As AI technology advances, related research is being 
actively discussed in the field of education. However, exist-
ing AI-related research has often focused on effectiveness, 
performance, learning outcomes, and practical applications 
of AI technologies (Chiu et al. 2023). While these studies 
have contributed to demonstrating the potential for educa-
tional applications of AI, the ethical, social, and identity 
issues have received relatively less attention. In particular, 
the emergence of generative AI raises the need for new dis-
cussions, as AI goes beyond being a mere tool and directly 
participates in content creation and (Schreiber, 2024; Kari-
yawasam 2020).
In this context, existing research on AI ethics has largely 
been discussed from a principle-based perspective, focusing 
on fairness, transparency, accountability, and explainability 
(Floridi 2019; Hagendorff 2022 ; Munn 2023; Wiese et al. 
2025). These discussions are grounded in concerns that AI 
may cause broader societal issues such as labor displace -
ment, inequality, and the spread of misinformation (Nguyen 
and Hekman 2024). However, these discussions have pri -
marily focused on norms and principles at the system level, 
while the ways in which such ethical issues are experienced 
and interpreted by students, particularly in creative contexts, 
remain less examined.
Generative AI differs from previous technologies in 
which it intervenes in domains traditionally considered 
uniquely human, such as creativity and authorship. These 
characteristics lead to the perception of AI not merely as a 
technical tool, but as an active role in the creative process,

which can create tensions (Schreiber, 2024). Consequently, 
AI-based creation shifts the focus from simply using tech-
nology to examining how creation is defined and understood. 
One of the central questions in AI-generated content is the 
issue of authorship, particularly the question of “who is the 
creator” (Hristov 2016; Huang et al. 2022). Although AI 
can make substantial contributions to the creative process, 
it is generally not recognized as an author under current 
academic and legal standards (Lund and Naheem 2024). 
As a result, the boundaries of contribution and responsi-
bility among humans, AI systems, and developers become 
increasingly blurred (Hajibayova and Lee 2025). In addi-
tion, while discussions are underway regarding declaration 
policies specifying the use of AI and authorship attribution 
standards, consistent standards have not yet been sufficiently 
established in the contexts of creative writing and educa-
tion (Hajibayova and Lee 2025; Lund and Naheem 2024). 
In addition, AI-generated content presents significant chal-
lenges to existing copyright systems. Issues such as owner-
ship of AI-generated outputs, the use of training data, and 
the difficulty of determining originality remain unresolved. 
Given that AI-generated content is created based on existing 
data, the debate over whether such results can be regarded 
as genuine creation continues (Hajibayova and Lee 2025).
These ethical considerations are not limited to general 
issues but are closely related to learners’ experiences in AI-
based art education. When learners perceive AI as replac-
ing human creativity, they may experience concerns about 
a loss of control (Jiang et al. 2024 ; Kang and Lou 2022 ). 
Therefore, ethical issues in AI-based art education need to 
be understood, as they are closely connected to technology 
acceptance, agency, and identity formation, particularly in 
how learners interpret their roles and contributions (Jiang 
et al. 2024; Kang and Lou 2022). However, research on 
learners’ ethical tensions and perceptions of authorship in 
AI-based creative environments remains limited, particularly 
in terms of how these experiences connect to attitudes and 
reactions toward AI and students’ future career.
2.5  Research gap
Rapid advancements in AI are transforming creative work 
across disciplines, influencing not only how creative prod-
ucts are generated but also how creators understand their 
roles within the process. While AI-integrated tools increas-
ingly mediate artistic production, far less is known about 
how sustained engagement with AI influences students’ 
evolving sense of themselves as creators.
Existing research has largely focused on the functional 
affordances of AI in creative contexts, such as increased effi-
ciency, productivity, and idea generation. Studies frequently 
examine technical skill acquisition, workflow optimiza-
tion, or learning outcomes associated with AI-supported 
environments (Chiu et al. 2023). However, these instrumen-
tal perspectives overlook a deeper and more complex dimen-
sion: how collaboration with AI negotiates students’ creative 
identity, perceived autonomy, and authorship.
From the perspective of identity formation theory (Erik -
son 1980; 1988), identity is not a fixed trait, but a dynamic 
construct shaped through social interaction and environmen-
tal engagement. As students interact with AI systems that 
generate, suggest, and modify creative outputs, they may 
renegotiate their self-concept as creators. AI may be posi-
tioned as a neutral tool, an active collaborator, or even a 
competing agent that challenges human originality. These 
shifting positions can influence students’ sense of agency, 
creative ownership, and long-term career outlook.
Furthermore, while prior research has examined self-effi-
cacy in technology-enhanced learning, limited attention has 
been paid to how AI-supported creative experiences simulta-
neously affect technical confidence, creative autonomy, and 
broader career perceptions. The tension between increas-
ing AI competence and emerging concerns about job dis-
placement, skill erosion, or copyright and authorship raises 
important questions about how students reconcile empower-
ment with uncertainty.
In addition, ethical concerns surrounding AI-generated 
art, including questions of originality, intellectual property, 
and the encroachment of machine systems into uniquely 
human domains, remain underexplored in relation to stu-
dents’ identity negotiation processes. How students inter -
pret these ethical tensions may shape not only their attitudes 
toward AI but also their willingness to embrace or resist 
AI-integrated creative practices.
To address these gaps, the present study examines how 
students conceptualize AI within their creative processes and 
how these perceptions relate to changes in self-efficacy, crea-
tive autonomy, and career outlook. By integrating quantita-
tive measures of AI-related competence and perception with 
qualitative analyses of students’ reflections and classroom 
interactions, this study offers a multidimensional account of 
how AI participation intersects with creator identity forma-
tion in educational settings.
3  Methods
3.1  Participants and contexts
Participating students in this study were 84 tenth-grade stu-
dents enrolled in a public high school located in a metro -
politan city in South Korea. The participants included 21 
male and 63 female students, aged 15 to 16 years. Most 
students reported little or no prior experience with AI-gen-
erated images (n = 75, 89.3%). The participating school is a 
specialized arts-focused high school with an emphasis on

animation, media creation, and related fields. The school 
emphasizes nurturing creative individuals who integrate 
imagination, artistic sensibility, and intellectual growth. 
Accordingly, many students at the school demonstrate strong 
interests in creative production and artistic expression.
The classroom implementation was led by one high 
school teacher with 23 years of teaching experience. The 
teacher has extensive expertise in educational technology 
and has actively integrated digital tools into her instructional 
practice. She is also the author of a practice-oriented book 
on AI integration for teachers, reflecting her professional 
experience and leadership in technology-enhanced teaching.
Institutional Review Board approval was obtained from 
the authors’ institution. All participants provided informed 
consent for participation and academic publication, includ-
ing authorization to use survey responses, interview data, 
and photographs of classroom activities.
3.2  Classroom curriculum
The curriculum was developed using a design-based research 
approach, characterized by iterative design, implementation, 
and refinement in collaboration with classroom teachers and 
the research team (Design-Based Research Collective 2003) 
using the  Lee and Yu (2026) framework. This approach 
enabled the development of a learning environment that was 
both theoretically grounded and responsive to the realities of 
classroom practice. The intervention was implemented over 
the course of one academic semester and consisted of 10 
weekly instructional sessions. Across the semester, the cur-
riculum was intentionally structured to progressively support 
students’ development of AI understanding, creative appli-
cation, and critical reflection. To support implementation 
consistency, all sessions followed a common instructional 
sequence and used the same core learning activities, AI 
tools, and project structure within the same course context.
In the early phase of the intervention, the first three ses-
sions focused on building foundational knowledge of AI. 
During this period, students were introduced to core con-
cepts underlying AI systems, including how generative AI 
produces outputs and processes information. Following 
this, session four shifted toward future-oriented thinking, 
encouraging students to explore the potential impact of 
AI on society and creative industries. These sessions also 
invited students to reflect on possible future career paths in 
relation to emerging technologies. Next, spanning sessions 
five through seven, centered on hands-on creative practice. 
Students engaged with generative AI tools such as Canva 
and Adobe Express to produce visual artifacts, with par -
ticular emphasis placed on experimentation, iteration, and 
the integration of AI into their creative workflows. In the 
later phase, sessions eight and nine were dedicated to stu-
dent presentations and peer feedback. Students shared their 
AI-assisted creative works, articulated their creative pro-
cesses, and reflected on the role of AI within their projects. 
The final session served as a wrap-up, providing structured 
opportunities for guided reflection on their overall learn-
ing experiences, including their evolving perceptions of AI, 
their identities as creators, and related ethical considerations. 
Overall, the curriculum was designed not only to enhance 
students’ technical competence in using AI tools but also to 
foster a deeper critical understanding of AI’s role in creative 
work, support the development of their emerging creator 
identities, and cultivate ethical awareness in AI-mediated 
contexts (Figs.  1, 2).
3.3  Research design and data source
This study employed a mixed-methods design to examine 
how students’ perceptions, identity, and ethical reason-
ing related to AI evolved through participation in an AI-
integrated art course (Creswell and Creswell 2017 ). A 
convergent design was adopted, in which quantitative and 
qualitative data were collected within the same instructional 
context and integrated during interpretation to provide a 
comprehensive understanding of students’ experiences. 
Quantitative findings were used to identify broad patterns 
in students’ AI-related perceptions, while qualitative find-
ings were used to contextualize and explain those patterns.
Quantitative data were collected through pre- and post-
surveys. The survey measured four constructs: students’ 
self-efficacy in AI knowledge and skills, perceptions of AI’s

To complement the survey data and capture the complex-
ity of students’ experiences, multiple sources of qualitative 
data were collected. Classroom observations were conducted 
throughout the intervention and documented through field 
notes focused on students’ interactions with AI tools, par -
ticipation patterns, creative decision-making, and emerging 
ethical concerns. In the final session, all students completed 
open-ended written reflection prompts about their expe-
riences with AI-assisted artmaking, their perceptions of 
AI’s role in creative work, their identities as creators, and 
related ethical considerations. In addition, semi-structured 
focus group interviews were conducted with 28 voluntary 
participants across five classes, with five to seven students 
participating from each class. Each focus group lasted 
approximately 30 min and was conducted after the final 
reflection session to further explore students’ experiences, 
perceptions, and reasoning. Student artifacts, including AI-
assisted creative outputs were collected and reviewed as 
supplementary contextual evidence. These artifacts helped 
contextualize how students integrated AI tools into their 
creative processes. These multiple quantitative and qualita-
tive data sources enabled triangulation and provided a richer 
understanding of how students experienced and interpreted 
AI in creative contexts.
3.4  Data analysis and trustworthiness
Quantitative data from the pre- and post-surveys were ana-
lyzed using paired-samples t-tests to examine changes in 
students’ AI-related perceptions and self-efficacy. Before 
conducting the paired-samples t-tests, the normality of 
the difference scores was examined, and no substantial 
violations were identified. Qualitative data were analyzed 
using thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006). The 
analysis followed a hybrid inductive-deductive approach. 
Initial coding was guided by the study’s conceptual focus 
on creator identity, agency, and ethical awareness, while 
additional codes were developed inductively from stu-
dents’ written reflections, focus group interviews, and 
observational notes. Three researchers participated in the 
coding process. The researchers first reviewed the qualita-

was reached. Themes were refined by comparing codes 
across data sources, revisiting representative excerpts, and 
ensuring coherence within themes and clear distinctions 
across themes.
Particular attention was given to how students described 
AI as a tool, collaborator, or source of uncertainty, and 
how these conceptualizations related to their percep-

To enhance trustworthiness, analytic memos and an audit 
trail were maintained throughout the coding process to 
document initial codes, code revisions, theme develop-

team was involved in the design and implementation of the 
AI-integrated art curriculum, reflexivity was addressed by 
documenting researcher assumptions and checking emerg-

4  Results
4.1  Changes in AI perceptions and self‑efficacy
To examine changes in students’ AI-related perceptions 
and self-efficacy, paired-samples t-tests were conducted 
comparing pre- and post-survey responses. Before con-
ducting the paired-samples t-tests, the normality of the 
difference scores was examined. No substantial violations 
were identified. Table  1 shows the results across four 
domains.
Results indicate statistically significant improve-
ments in three of the four AI self-efficacy items. Students 
reported increased ability to explain how AI generates 
outputs (Item 2; t =  − 2.345, p = 0.023), understand how 
AI recognizes images and language (Item 3; t  =  − 2.312, 
p = 0.025, d = 0.25), and effectively use AI-based programs 
or applications (Item 4; t  =  − 2.542, p = 0.014, d = 0.28). 
Although students’ confidence in using AI to obtain 
desired results (Item 1) increased from pre- to post-test, 
the change was not statistically significant (t  =  − 1.659, 
p = 0.103). Overall, these findings suggest that participa-
tion in the AI-integrated learning experience was associ-
ated with strengthened procedural and conceptual under -
standing of AI technologies.
Students’ beliefs about AI’s potential to replace 
human jobs (Item 5) slightly decreased from pre- to 
post-test, though this change was not statistically sig-
nificant (t  = 1.815, p = 0.075). Similarly, perceptions that 
AI would significantly impact human occupations (Item 
6) remained largely unchanged (t  =  − 0.198, p = 0.844). 
These results suggest that while students developed greater 
AI competence, their broader beliefs about AI’s societal 
and occupational impact remained relatively stable.
No statistically significant changes were observed in 
students’ ethical perceptions of AI-assisted creative work 
(Items 7 & 8). Both the pre- and post-surveys indicate that 
students consistently viewed art as an inherently human 
domain. Agreement with the view that uniquely human 
domains such as art should not be encroached upon by AI 
remained stable. These findings underscore the persistence 
of students’ deeply held belief that artistic creation should 
remain uniquely human.
Students’ overall career optimism (Item 9) did not 
change significantly (t  = 0.248, p = 0.805). However, per-
sistence in the face of career challenges (Item 10) showed 
a statistically significant decrease (t  = 2.388, p = 0.020, 
d = 0.26), indicating a slight reduction in self-reported 
perseverance following the intervention. The decrease in 
Item 10 shows that, as students developed a more con-
crete understanding of AI’s potential impact, they may 
have engaged in more critical reflection about their future 
careers.
These findings indicate that while the AI-integrated 
learning experience enhanced students’ technical compe-
tence, it did not lead to corresponding changes in their 
broader beliefs or attitudes. Instead, the results point to 
emerging tensions related to creator identity, ethical judg-
ment, and future career expectations. To further examine 
these tensions, the following sections draw on qualitative 
data to explore how students make sense of the role of AI 
in creative processes (ontology), their identity as creators 
(agency), and the ethical implications of AI-assisted crea -
tion (ethics).

4.2  Conceptual typologies of AI perception 
in creative work
To address the second research question, this study exam-
ined how students conceptualize the role of AI in the crea-
tive process. Analysis of student responses revealed three 
dominant orientations: AI as a tool, a collaborative partner, 
and a potential threat (Fig. 3). These categories are not mutu-
ally exclusive but rather represent a continuum of perception 
through which students interpret AI’s role in creative work.
4.2.1  Tool‑oriented perspectives: AI as a productivity tool
A substantial number of students conceptualized AI primar-
ily as a functional tool that enhances efficiency and reduces 
the burden of repetitive or time-consuming tasks. In this 
view, AI is positioned as an assistive mechanism that sup-
ports, rather than alters, the creative process. Students fre-
quently emphasized speed, convenience, and the ability to 
externalize mental imagery:
“With just a few words, it quickly and conveniently 
created the drawings I wanted.” (Student ID 1108)
“It helped visualize the images I was imagining 
quickly.” (Student ID 1219)
Others highlighted AI’s role in offloading tedious pre-
paratory work:
“It handles tasks like sketching outlines or basic 
groundwork that I usually find tedious, which saves 
time and makes the process more efficient.” (Student 
ID 1502)
“AI helps with tasks that are close to manual labor, 
making the process easier.” (Student ID 1521)
In addition, some students associated AI with improved 
output quality:
“AI can be a tool that produces higher-quality 
results.” (Student ID 1401)
These responses position AI as an instrumental 
extension of human capability, where efficiency gains 
are foregrounded while authorship remains firmly 
human-centered.
4.2.2  Partner‑oriented perspectives: AI as a creative 
partner
Beyond a purely instrumental role, many students 
described AI as an active collaborator that contributes to 
idea generation and creative exploration. In this orienta -
tion, AI is not merely executing tasks but participating 
in the creative process by offering suggestions, feedback, 
and unexpected outputs. Students described using AI as a 
source of inspiration:
“Sometimes, when I struggle with inspiration, I 
enter keywords into AI and give specific commands. 
It helps me brainstorm ideas for stories or illustra-
tions.” (Student ID 1425)
“There were moments when I got unexpectedly good 
ideas from AI.” (Student ID 1529)
Others framed AI as a supportive collaborator:
“AI can act as a supporter that provides feedback and 
ideas.” (Student ID 1129)
“Humans and AI can collaborate to create better 
work.” (Student ID 1318)
In this perspective, creativity is understood as a dis-
tributed process, where human intention and machine-
generated suggestions interact dynamically. AI is thus 
positioned as a co-creative agent that expands, rather than 
replaces, human ideation.

4.2.3  Threat‑oriented perspectives: AI as a threat 
to creativity and human labor
In contrast, a third group of students expressed concern, 
discomfort, or even strong resistance toward AI in creative 
contexts. These perceptions framed AI as a disruptive force 
that may undermine creativity, human agency, and the value 
of artistic labor. Some students described an initial sense of 
awe accompanied by unease:
“It was fascinating to use AI for the first time, but also 
a bit scary to see how advanced it already is.” (Student 
ID 1306)
Others articulated concerns about the pace of technologi-
cal advancement:
“The speed at which AI is developing is almost fright-
ening, and it seems difficult for individuals to keep 
up.” (Student ID 1231)
A recurring theme was the fear that efficiency may come 
at the expense of artistic depth:
“I’m worried that in creative fields, efficiency will 
increase while artistic value decreases.” (Student ID 
1432)
Students also expressed concerns about dependency and 
skill erosion:
“If I use AI too much, I might forget how to come up 
with ideas on my own.” (Student ID 1310)
Finally, several students raised concerns about labor dis-
placement and the devaluation of human artists:
“If AI can create professional-quality illustrations in 
seconds, why would anyone pay an artist?” (Student 
ID 1409)
“If AI keeps improving, will there still be a need for 
human artists?” (Student ID 1406)
These responses reflect broader anxieties about automa-
tion, suggesting that students perceive AI not only as a tool 
or partner but also as a potential competitor that challenges 
the sustainability of human-centered creative work.
4.3  Creator identity and perceived autonomy
To address the third research question, this study examined 
how AI integration negotiates students’ perceptions of crea-
tor identity and agency. Analysis revealed a continuum of 
perceived autonomy, ranging from strong human-centered 
control to diminished agency under AI influence (Fig.  4).
4.3.1  High autonomy: AI as assistive support
Students in this group maintained a strong sense of author -
ship and viewed AI as a supplementary tool that enhances 
efficiency without compromising creative control.
“I still make all the creative choices. AI just helps with 
small things, like generating references or basic color-
ing.” (Student ID 1547)
Some students explicitly rejected the idea that AI poses 
a significant threat:
“After using AI, I realized it’s not as powerful as I 
expected. The results weren’t always satisfying, and 
it didn’t feel like it could replace artists. It’s useful 
for simple tasks, but not a real substitute.” (Student 
ID 1231)
For these students, creativity remains fundamentally 
human-driven, with AI serving as a limited augmentation 
tool.
4.3.2  Moderate autonomy: AI as co‑creator
Students in the moderate autonomy group described a more 
entangled relationship with AI, where creative control is 
shared, negotiated, and sometimes dependent on AI-gener -
ated suggestions.
“AI helped structure my ideas, but I still edit what it 
gives me, and the final decision is mine.” (Student ID 
1309)
Here, authorship becomes collaborative. While students 
retain ultimate control, they acknowledge that AI meaning-
fully shapes the direction and development of their work.

4.3.3  Low autonomy: AI as replacement
Students with low perceived autonomy expressed concern 
that AI may displace their role as creators or diminish their 
creative agency.
“I used to draw everything from my imagination. 
Now I start with AI, and it creates things for me. I’m 
not sure if that makes me a better artist or a lazier 
one.” (Student ID 1107)
“I feel anxious that rapid advancements in AI may 
prevent me from securing a job in the future.” (Stu-
dent ID 1407)
This perspective reflects anxiety about over-reliance 
on AI and the potential erosion of foundational creative 
skills. For these students, AI introduces tension into their 
identity as creators, raising questions about authenticity, 
effort, and ownership.
4.4  Ethical tensions and identity negotiation
The fourth research question examined how ethical con-
cerns surrounding AI intersect with students’ acceptance 
of AI and their self-perception as future creators. Findings 
indicate that ethical considerations are deeply intertwined 
with identity formation and significantly shape students’ 
attitudes toward AI.
A central concern involved authorship and originality. 
Students questioned whether AI-generated outputs could 
be legitimately attributed to themselves:
“AI-generated art looks too similar to real artists’ 
work, which makes me uncomfortable and feels 
unethical.” (Student ID 1526)
“What AI creates cannot be considered art. It feels 
like mass-produced results rather than genuine crea-
tion.” (Student ID 1104)
Transparency in AI training data emerged as another 
major issue:
“We need transparent processes, especially about 
how AI learns from data and existing artwork.” (Stu -
dent ID 1422)
“AI is trained on other people’s work. When I use 
it, am I unknowingly stealing?” (Student ID 1513)
Some students articulated strong ethical rejection of AI 
in creative contexts:
“Training AI on copyrighted artwork without con-
sent is not acceptable. Machines cannot engage in 
artistic practice.” (Student ID 1327)
“Using other artists’ work through AI to generate 
outputs is not art. It feels unethical.” (Student ID 
1235)
Others framed AI use as a form of unfair advantage:
“In situations where people should be evaluated based 
on their own abilities, using AI is like cheating.” (Stu-
dent ID 1134)
Beyond legal concerns, students also questioned whether 
AI can possess the essential qualities of creativity:
“AI doesn’t have feelings, memories, or experiences. 
How can it create real art?” (Student ID 1110)
“Please don’t describe AI as drawing.” (Student ID 
1528)
“The moment we give AI the status of a creator, the 
boundaries of art collapse.” (Student ID 1535)
These responses suggest that some students were hesitant 
to recognize AI as a legitimate creative agent. Ethical con-
cerns, therefore, are not merely external considerations but 
are central to how students define creativity, authorship, and 
their own identities as creators.
5  Discussion
This study examined how AI-integrated creative learning 
environments influence students’ self-efficacy, perceptions 
of AI, and the formation of creator identity. By integrat-
ing quantitative and qualitative findings, the results reveal 
a complex pattern: while students developed stronger AI-
related competencies, their perceptions of creativity, author-
ship, and future careers became more nuanced.
5.1  AI competence and acceptance
The quantitative findings demonstrate clear gains in AI-
related self-efficacy, particularly in students’ understanding 
of how AI systems function and their ability to use AI tools 
effectively. These results suggest that the instructional inter-
vention successfully supported both conceptual and proce-
dural engagement with AI (Lee and Kwon 2024).
However, although students increased their AI knowl-
edge, these gains did not translate into more optimistic per-
ceptions of AI’s societal impact or positive attitudes toward 
AI-generated artwork. This implies that AI competence does 
not necessarily correspond to AI acceptance, since they 
may be influenced by various contextual factors (Jiang et al. 
2024; Shin 2026a). Students’ beliefs about AI’s influence on 
jobs remained largely unchanged, and notably, career persis-
tence decreased significantly. This divergence suggests that 
increased competence does not lead to increased confidence

in the future. Instead, greater exposure to AI may prompt 
more critical and realistic reflections on career stability and 
competition.
The survey findings suggest that AI education increased 
students’ technical competence without leading to uncritical 
acceptance of AI in creative work. While students demon-
strated significant gains in self-efficacy in AI skills, their 
broader perceptions of AI remained differentiated and, in 
some cases, resistant. Notably, students’ agreement with 
statements suggesting that AI would fully replace human 
jobs decreased after hands-on experience, whereas their 
belief that AI would have a substantial impact on occupa-
tions remained consistently high. These patterns indicate 
that students came to distinguish between technological 
replacement and technological influence, recognizing AI as 
unlikely to fully substitute human creators while still capa-
ble of reshaping work processes, particularly in terms of 
efficiency and task distribution.
5.2  From AI as Tool to AI as ontological question
Qualitative findings further reveal that students do not hold 
a single, unified perception of AI. Instead, they position AI 
along a continuum as a tool, a collaborator, or a threat. These 
varying conceptualizations reflect not only functional inter-
pretations but also deeper ontological stances regarding the 
nature of creativity and authorship.
Importantly, these positions are not merely descriptive 
but shape how students engage with AI in practice. Viewing 
AI as a tool maintains a clear boundary between human and 
machine, whereas seeing AI as a partner introduces a more 
distributed model of creativity. In contrast, perceiving AI as 
a threat reflects a breakdown of this boundary, where AI is 
seen as encroaching upon or replacing human creative roles.
5.3  Negotiation of creator identity and career 
persistence
These differing perceptions of AI roles are closely tied to 
variations in perceived agency and autonomy. Students 
who viewed AI as a tool maintained strong agency and a 
stable sense of authorship, while those who perceived AI 
as a collaborator experienced shared or negotiated control. 
In contrast, students who viewed AI as a threat reported 
diminished autonomy and expressed concern about losing 
their role as creators.
This continuum suggests that AI integration did not 
appear to uniformly enhance or diminish students’ sense of 
agency; rather, it introduced new conditions under which 
students perceived and negotiated their agency in AI-assisted 
creative work. Students are not simply adopting AI tools 
but actively renegotiating what it means to be a creator in 
an AI-mediated environment. In this sense, AI appeared to 
serve as a context through which students reflected on and 
negotiated their roles and identity as creators in AI-assisted 
creative processes (Erikson 1988; Katz and Aakhus 2002), 
prompting reflection on authorship (Hristov 2016; Huang 
et al. 2022), originality, and the value of human contribu-
tion (Hajibayova and Lee 2025). Identity formation theory 
suggests that adolescents’ sense of self develops through 
interaction with social and technological contexts (Erikson 
1988; Katz and Aakhus 2002). For aspiring artists, working 
with AI raises fundamental questions such as “Am I still the 
creator?” and “What does authorship mean in AI-assisted 
art?”.
Interestingly, students reported a decrease in uncondi-
tional career persistence in the survey, indicating a shift 
away from idealized narratives of perseverance. Rather than 
signaling diminished motivation, this change may reflect 
increased realism about the evolving structure of creative 
work in an AI-influenced context. As students recognized 
that AI may not eliminate creative professions but could 
alter entry pathways and redistribute routine labor, they may 
have begun to reconsider a creative career. In this sense, AI 
education appears to have fostered reflective reconsidera-
tion rather than resignation, supporting a view of learning 
that emphasizes critical awareness over compliance. While 
students gain technical competence, they also encounter 
challenges to their assumptions about creativity, author -
ship, and future careers. The observed decrease in career 
persistence, therefore, should not be interpreted as a nega -
tive outcome or instructional failure. Rather, it may reflect 
a process of reflection, questioning, and negotiation regard-
ing AI-assisted creativity and authorship, in which students 
move from simplified or optimistic assumptions toward more 
complex and critical understandings of AI’s role in society. 
This tension may be a necessary condition for deeper learn-
ing. Instead of reinforcing uncritical optimism, effective AI 
education may require creating spaces where students can 
grapple with uncertainty, ambiguity, and ethical complexity.
5.4  Ethical tensions of identity formation
Ethical concerns emerged as a central dimension of this 
identity negotiation process. This concern aligns with 
broader debates in AI ethics and intellectual property author-
ship (Floridi 2019; Hajibayova and Lee 2025). Students’ 
ethical and artistic boundaries remained firmly intact. Stu-
dents had low acceptance for AI-created output as arts in 
both pre- and post-survey. This resistance aligns with the 
lack of change observed in survey items related to the irre-
placeability of human creativity and the ethical legitimacy of 
AI-generated art. Following the interviews, students posed 
questions about authorship, copyright, and the legitimacy of 
AI-generated work, often linking these concerns directly to 
their own identity as creators.

Numerous students resisted attributing creative agency to 
AI, emphasizing the importance of human intention, expe-
rience, and emotional depth. These responses suggest that 
ethical considerations are not peripheral but foundational to 
how students define creativity itself. The rejection of AI as 
a legitimate creator reflects an attempt to preserve bounda-
ries between human and machine in the face of increasingly 
blurred distinctions.
5.5  Implications for research in AI education
Our findings highlight the importance of distinguishing 
between competence and endorsement in AI education 
research. While students demonstrated significant gains in 
technical understanding and skills, these gains did not trans-
late into uncritical acceptance of AI as a legitimate creative 
agent. Instead, students engaged with AI in a selective and 
critical manner, using it pragmatically while maintaining 
clear ethical and identity-based boundaries. This suggests 
that increased competence may, in some cases, heighten 
critical awareness rather than promote acceptance. These 
findings challenge implicit assumptions in AI education 
that skill development naturally leads to positive attitudes 
toward AI. Future research should therefore move beyond 
linear models of learning outcomes and examine the com-
plex, and at times conflicting, relationships among compe-
tence, identity, and ethical judgment in AI-mediated learning 
environments.
5.6  Implications for art education in the age of AI
Our study highlights that AI-integrated instruction should 
move beyond a sole focus on technical skill development. 
Our findings suggest design principles and core components 
for AI-integrated art education (Fig.  5). In the context of 
art education, this involves a more comprehensive approach 
that addresses multiple dimensions of learning. First, foun-
dational artistic skills and knowledge remain essential. AI 
should not replace core artistic competencies but rather build 
upon them. Second, students must develop active agency 
in working with AI. This includes not only learning how 
to use AI tools, but also how to direct, critique, and inten-
tionally integrate AI into their creative processes. Third, art 
education should explicitly address creator identity. As AI 
becomes embedded in creative workflows, students need 
structured opportunities to reflect on authorship, ownership, 
and what it means to create in collaboration with non-human 
agents. Finally, ethical understanding must be treated as a 
core component rather than an add-on. Students’ awareness 
of issues such as copyright and data use functions as a criti-
cal lens through which they evaluate the legitimacy of AI-
generated work.
These findings call for a reorientation of art education 
toward balancing artistic skill, human agency, identity for -
mation, and ethical reasoning. Without these components, 
students may develop technical competence without the con-
ceptual and ethical frameworks needed to navigate AI-rich 
environments. These findings highlight the need for a more 
holistic model of AI-integrated art education that balances 
art skill development with identity formation and AI ethical 
understanding.
5.7  Implications for career education
This study contributes to emerging discussions on career 
sensemaking in the age of AI. Students did not perceive AI 
merely as a classroom tool, but as a force with the poten-
tial to influence creative labor and future career trajecto-
ries. Notably, increased exposure to AI was associated with 
reduced career persistence, suggesting that learning about AI 
may introduce uncertainty alongside adaptability. Students’ 
responses reflected a dual process: growing awareness of 
AI’s capabilities alongside concerns about their own future 
roles. These findings suggest that career education should 
move beyond promoting adaptability alone and instead 
support students in navigating ambiguity, uncertainty, and 
questions about their roles in rapidly evolving creative work 
environments.
6  Conclusion
This research reveals a deep ambivalence toward AI in 
creative work. Students recognize AI’s potential for effi-
ciency, inspiration, and collaboration, but they also strug-
gle with issues of identity, career pathways, and ethics.

While some students see AI as an enhancement to their 
creative process, others feel it undermines their role as 
artists. These results suggest that AI literacy education 
must extend beyond technical proficiency. Schools must 
incorporate critical discussions on authorship, ethics, and 
career adaptability, helping students develop a balanced 
perspective on AI’s role in creativity. Future research 
should explore how these attitudes evolve over time and 
what pedagogical strategies can best support students in 
integrating AI without compromising their creative iden-
tity. As AI continues to redefine artistic production, educa-
tors must ensure that young creators retain their agency, 
refine their artistic skills, and critically engage with the 
ethical challenges of AI-assisted creativity. Without such 
guidance, there is a risk that students will either over-rely 
on AI to the detriment of their own development or reject 
it entirely due to uncertainty and fear. A nuanced and well-
structured approach to AI education will be essential in 
preparing students for a future where human and machine 
creativity coexist. The future research needs to implement 
classroom intervention with new curricula combined eth-
ics and creator reformation in AI-assisted art education.
