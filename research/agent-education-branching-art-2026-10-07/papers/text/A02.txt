Systematic Review
Generative AI in Art Education: A Systematic Review of
Research Trends, Tool Applications, and Outcomes (2019–2025)
Yihan Jiang *
 , Yujiao Fan
 and Zifeng Liu
School of Teaching and Learning, College of Education, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL 32611, USA;
yujiaofan@ufl.edu (Y.F.); liuzifeng@ufl.edu (Z.L.)
* Correspondence: jiangyihan@ufl.edu
Abstract
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools are transforming art education by enabling
instant creation of textual, visual, audio, and multimodal outputs. This systematic re-
view synthesizes research on GenAI applications in art education from January 2019 to
August 2025. Following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, 19 peer-reviewed empirical studies
across six databases (Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Springer, Taylor & Francis, Scopus,
and ERIC) met inclusion criteria, which required clear pedagogical implementation with
students or educators as active participants. Research accelerated from two studies in
2023 to 14 in 2025, with most studies examining higher education and East Asia contexts
through mixed methods approaches and grounded in constructivist and cognitive learn-
ing theories. Text-to-image generation models (DALL-E, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion)
and conversational AI (ChatGPT) were most frequently implemented across creative pro-
duction, pedagogical scaffolding, and instructional design applications. Findings from
this emerging body of research suggest that GenAI has the potential to improve learning
achievement, creative thinking, engagement, and cultural understanding when integrated
through structured pedagogical frameworks with intentional instructor design. However,
these positive outcomes represent early-stage implementation trends in well-resourced
contexts rather than broadly generalizable conclusions. Successful integration requires
explicit instructional frameworks, clear ethical guidelines for human-AI collaboration, and
evolved assessment methods.
Keywords: generative artificial intelligence; GenAI; art education; systematic review;
pedagogical integration; creative learning; educational technology
1. Introduction
1.1. Art Education and Technology Integration
Art education helps students build creative expression, visual literacy, critical thinking,
and problem-solving through fine and visual art practices (Dobbs, 1998; Freedman, 2025;
E. Zimmerman, 2009). As educational contexts increasingly incorporate digital tools and
technologies, art education has become a site for examining how emerging technologies
intersect with fundamental pedagogical goals (Black & Browning, 2011). Over time, teachers
have brought different technologies into art classrooms. They have used slide projectors
to show famous artworks, digital cameras to record projects, and software such as Adobe
Photoshop for image editing. Tablets have also been used for digital drawing (Tillander,
2011). In most cases, these digital tools have supported teaching and learning rather
than replaced the creative process itself. Students still made the main artistic choices and

carried out their ideas (Bardzell, 2007; Jones, 1986). Yet, these tools have several limits.
They take time and training to use well. They give little help with idea development
or concept exploration. They do not do much for students who face creative blocks or
technical difficulties. They also create extra costs for teachers and schools (Alawad, 2013).
Prior research on technology integration in art education has examined diverse platforms
from traditional digital tools to augmented and virtual reality (González-Zamar & Abad-
Segura, 2020; Huang et al., 2016). However, the rapid emergence of AI tools presents
a fundamentally different technological landscape, requiring fresh examination of how
educators can thoughtfully integrate these tools.
1.2. Generative AI: Distinguishing from Traditional AI in Education
Research on artificial intelligence (AI) in education has primarily focused on applica-
tions such as profiling and prediction, assessment and evaluation, adaptive systems and
personalization, and intelligent tutoring systems (Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). Mallik and
Gangopadhyay (2023) categorize AI in education into proactive and reactive engagement.
Most applications focus on analyzing learner data to provide feedback or predictions,
without contributing to creative knowledge generation. Generative artificial intelligence
(GenAI) represents a distinct category of AI that moves beyond responsive optimization
to active content creation (Samala et al., 2025). GenAI encompasses machine learning sys-
tems capable of generating novel content, including text, images, audio, and multimodal
outputs from natural language prompts. Tools like ChatGPT, DALL-E, Midjourney, and
Stable Diffusion, released in the early 2020s, address many limits of older technologies.
They can create complex visuals in seconds, help students brainstorm ideas, and reduce
the technical skills needed to produce high-quality work (Palani & Ramos, 2024; Park,
2023). This change reshapes how people think about artistic learning and creation (Torres
Carceller, 2024). For example, a student who cannot draw well can still make detailed
artwork with text prompts, while a chatbot can give real-time feedback on style, concept,
or art history. However, these capabilities introduce unprecedented challenges. Ethically,
questions emerge around authorship, copyright, and the environmental impact of training
large models (Blair, 2025; Garcia, 2024; Leonard, 2021). Pedagogically, concerns arise about
diminished skill development when students bypass traditional practice stages, potential
aesthetic homogenization as GenAI models reflect training data biases, and the question of
what constitutes meaningful artistic learning when production processes become partially
automated (Aksoy & Kursun, 2024; Doshi & Hauser, 2023; Holmes & Zhgenti, 2024; Mel-
chior & Farinosi, 2025). GenAI therefore opens creative access to more people and offers
new ways to teach art. At the same time, it questions long-held values in art education
that have focused on process, skill, and personal growth rather than the final product
(Stewart, 2019).
1.3. The Literature Gap
Recent reviews have summarized how AI works and how it has been used in education
(Tahiru, 2021). Some focused on AI chatbots in classrooms (Labadze et al., 2023; ˙Ipek et al.,
2023), while others explored AI use across college subjects (Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019).
However, most of this research centers on STEM fields, leaving art education largely
unexplored. Studies on AI in art do exist but cover different areas. Some reviews have
discussed broad technologies such as AR, VR, and machine learning, or systems that
provide tutoring support (Adeel & Yasmeen, 2025). Others have examined how AI is
used for creating or analyzing art rather than for teaching art (Oksanen et al., 2023). A
few broader reviews have looked at AI’s role in creativity and audience engagement but
did not focus on classroom practices (Then et al., 2023). One review that specifically

focused on K-12 art education found that AI use in this area is still in its early stage (Su &

arts, the maturity level of this field remains low, with most research focuses on testing
strategies and developing tools rather than systematically applying learning theory to
guide implementation or examining teaching and learning outcomes (Su & Mokmin, 2024).
The emergence of GenAI tools adds another layer of complexity to this already un-
derdeveloped field. As of 2025, empirical research on GenAI in art education remains in
its nascent stages, with most available studies published within the past year. Reviews
on GenAI in general education have shown its strong potential for personalizing learning
and generating content (Mittal et al., 2024). They have also found wide use in higher
education and positive student attitudes toward these tools (Batista et al., 2024). Even
with these studies, a gap remains. No systematic review has yet explored how GenAI
tools are being used for teaching and learning in art education. Current research has not
examined which learning theories guide GenAI-based art instruction, what evidence shows
changes in student learning, or what recommendations teachers can follow when using
these tools. This gap is significant because art education embodies distinctive pedagogical
purposes that differ from STEM-oriented contexts where GenAI research has concentrated.
Art education prioritized: (1) iterative creative process over final product (E. Zimmerman,
2010), (2) development of aesthetic judgment and critical visual literacy (Crawford, 1987),
and (3) cultural understanding and authentic expression (Ballengee-Morris & Stuhr, 2001).
Art education also raises specific teaching concerns, such as how to keep the creative
process active, build basic artistic skills, support critical thinking about beauty and style,
and protect personal and cultural expression when using GenAI (Eri¸ sti & Freedman, 2024).
These issues have not yet been discussed in broader reviews that focus only on general
learning outcomes.
1.4. Research Questions and Objectives
This systematic review addresses this gap by synthesizing research on GenAI applica-
tions in art education from January 2019 to August 2025. To structure this synthesis, we
organized inquiry around four progressive research questions that move from descriptive
(what is happening?) through explanatory (how and why?) to prescriptive (what should
happen?) Specifically, this review investigates four research questions:
1. What are the temporal trends and research focus areas in GenAI art education
applications?
2. What specific GenAI tools are employed and how are they implemented within art
educational contexts?
3. What learning theoretical frameworks guide GenAI integration in art education?
4. What are the documented impacts and what practical recommendations emerge from
empirical investigations?
By systematically examining these dimensions, this review aims to provide art edu-
cators, curriculum developers, and educational policymakers with an evidence-informed
understanding of: (1) what is currently known about GenAI integration in art education
contexts, (2) what remains unknown and requires future investigation, and (3) what pro-
visional guidance the existing evidence base can support for thoughtful implementation.
This approach acknowledges both the genuine pedagogical promise GenAI offers and the
legitimate cautions regarding its effects on artistic authenticity, skill development, and
educational equity.

2. Materials and Methods
This systematic review was not prospectively registered. The study follows PRISMA
2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021) to ensure methodological rigor and transparency through-
out the review process. This section details the search strategy, eligibility criteria, and data
extraction and analysis methods employed in this review.
2.1. Search Strategy
To ensure systematic comprehensiveness within manageable scope, initial search
parameters were established: (1) language restriction to English-language peer-reviewed
publications, (2) temporal scope from January 2019 to August 2025, (3) database selection
of six platforms. These parameters were applied at the database level to constrain initial
retrieval. Six databases were searched: Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Springer, Taylor &
Francis, Scopus, and ERIC. These databases were selected for their coverage of education
technology, art education, and computer science literature.
Search terms were developed through preliminary scoping searches and team dis-
cussion to ensure broad yet focused retrieval. Two core concept clusters were identified:
(1) generative AI technologies, and (2) art education contexts. These concept clusters were
combined using the Boolean AND operator to retrieve documents addressing both concepts.

2.2. Eligibility Criteria
We developed our inclusion criteria based on Meline’s (2006) systematic review prin-
ciples. These criteria focus on research question relevance, methodological quality, and
pedagogical applicability, ensuring both internal and external validity of our review. The
inclusion criteria encompassed empirical investigations (quantitative, qualitative, and
mixed methods) conducted in art educational settings with systematic implementation of
GenAI tools. Eligible studies featured students or educators as active participants utilizing
GenAI for structured learning activities with defined objectives and analysis of educational
processes or outcomes. The exclusion criteria eliminated several categories of literature
across multiple dimensions. We excluded purely theoretical analyses and opinion pieces
without empirical foundation, as our focus required evidence-based research. Studies
involving participants other than students or educators in educational contexts were ex-
cluded, as were commercial design applications outside educational settings. Critically, we
excluded studies that merely offered exploratory reflections or tool demonstrations without
systematic educational implementation. This included research lacking structured learning
activities, defined educational objectives, or systematic analysis of learning processes and
skill development impacts. Additionally, we excluded studies focusing on traditional
digital art tools rather than GenAI implementation, and those providing only superficial
observations without meaningful analysis of educational outcomes. The specific criteria
applied for study selection are presented in Table 2.

2.3. Study Selection and Quality Assessment Process
The study selection process followed standard PRISMA procedures across three phases:
identification, screening, and inclusion. In the identification phase, database searches across
six platforms yielded 598 records. Manual searching of reference lists and citation tracking
identified 2 additional records, bringing the initial pool to 600 publications. All identified
records were compiled and duplicate records were removed. This deduplication process
eliminated 32 duplicate records, resulting in 568 unique records for title and abstract
screening. In the screening phase, two independent reviewers screened all 568 titles and
abstracts against the eligibility criteria specified in Table 2. Reviewers worked in pairs,
with each record evaluated by both reviewers to ensure consistent application of criteria.
Records identified by both reviewers as potentially meeting inclusion criteria advanced
to full-text review. This title and abstract screening phase resulted in 52 reports identified
for retrieval. Both reviewers independently assessed the 52 full-text reports against the
eligibility criteria. Of these, 4 reports could not be obtained, leaving 48 reports for detailed
eligibility assessment. When reviewers disagreed about eligibility, disagreements were
resolved through detailed discussion examining the specific criteria of contention. When
consensus could not be reached through discussion, a third independent reviewer was
consulted to make the final determination. This process resulted in exclusion of 29 reports
for the reasons shown in Figure 1. This resulted in 19 studies meeting all inclusion criteria
for synthesis in the inclusion phase.
Subsequently, all 19 included studies underwent quality assessment using the Mixed
Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), version 2018 (Hong et al., 2018), a validated instrument
designed to evaluate methodological rigor across diverse study designs. The MMAT pro-
vides design-specific criteria for qualitative (5 items), quantitative (5 items), and mixed
methods (5 items) studies. Both independent reviewers applied the tool to each included
study, rating each criterion as “Yes,” “No,” or “Can’t tell” according to standardized guide-
lines. Inter-rater reliability between the two independent reviewers was high, with Cohen’s
Kappa κ = 0.868. Disagreements in quality ratings were resolved through detailed discus-
sion until consensus was reached. All included studies indicated sufficient methodological
quality. Eleven studies (57.9%) met all five quality criteria (100% quality score) for their
respective study designs. Six studies (31.6%) met four of five criteria (80% quality score).

or analysis, or presented theoretical discussions and tool demonstrations.
2.4. Data Extraction and Analysis Methods
Data extraction was conducted systematically by both reviewers to ensure accuracy
and completeness. For each included study, extracted data included: (1) publication demo-
graphics (year, geographic distribution, institutional affiliation type, publication channel),
(2) research focus (educational level, application scenario, theme, methodology, sample
size), (3) technological specifications (GenAI tools, applications), educational theoretical
https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci16010047

frameworks, and (4) outcome measures (impacts, recommendations). Institutional affilia-
tion types were categorized into single institution and multiple institutions. For regional
analysis, studies were classified based on the region of the first author’s primary (first-listed)
institutional affiliation to ensure consistency and avoid duplicate counting. Publication
venues were classified across journal articles and conference papers. Educational level
was categorized into three groups: K-12 education, higher education, and teacher educa-
tion/professional development. Methodologies were classified into three main categories:
quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods (Taherdoost, 2022).
Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (Kaur et al., 2018) and conventional
content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). Descriptive statistics examined distribution
patterns across nine dimensions: temporal trends, geographical distribution, publication
channels, institutional types, educational levels, methodology, sample size, application
scenarios, and GenAI tools in art education. Following the conventional content analysis
approach (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005), this review employed inductive coding to categorize
research focus (theme), technological specifications (GenAI applications), educational theo-
retical frameworks, and outcome measures (impacts and recommendations). Categories
emerged from the data through iterative reading and analysis of the included studies,
without imposing predetermined theoretical frameworks. All data extraction, coding,
and analysis of the included studies were conducted using Microsoft Excel (version 2023).
Figures were generated programmatically using Python (version 3.11.9) and the Matplotlib
library (version 3.10.3) at 300 dpi resolution.
3. Results
This section addresses the four research questions posed in this study, progressing
from the current landscape to implementation specifics, theoretical foundations, and impli-
cations. It represents an analysis of the currently available empirical literature on GenAI
in art education. We first examine temporal trends and research focus areas in GenAI
art education applications. Second, we identify the specific GenAI tools employed and
their implementations within art educational contexts. Third, we analyze the educational
theoretical frameworks guiding GenAI integration. Fourth, we summarize the documented
impacts of GenAI integration. Finally, we synthesize practical recommendations emerging
from empirical investigations.

3.1.5. Research Themes and Focus Areas
From a thematic perspective, the research themes reveal that studies predominantly
focus on: (1) Pedagogical integration and instructional innovation, encompassing studies
examining how GenAI tools can be effectively incorporated into art curricula through
various instructional approaches. This includes investigations of composition learning
integration (Ansone et al., 2025), scaffolding instruction (Ling et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025;
Zeng et al., 2025), flipped classroom models with AI-generated virtual instructors (Chen
et al., 2025a), prompt engineering and iterative creation processes (Hutson & Cotroneo,
2023a, 2023b), mind mapping approaches (Chiu & Hwang, 2025), color composition instruc-
tion (Ling et al., 2025a), and AI-assisted design communication in pedagogical practice
(Kang et al., 2025). (2) Cognitive and affective learning outcomes with studies investigating
GenAI‘s impact on multiple dimensions of student development. These include creativity
and creative self-efficacy (Chiu & Hwang, 2025; Kicklighter et al., 2024; Xu, 2025; Zeng
et al., 2025), critical thinking abilities (Chiu & Hwang, 2025), learning motivation and
engagement (Chen et al., 2025b; Lee et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2025), self-efficacy (Chen et al.,
2025b; Hu et al., 2025), academic achievement and learning performance (Chen et al., 2025a,
2025b; Hu et al., 2025), and problem-solving capabilities (Hu et al., 2025). (3) Cultural
heritage education and reproduction explore GenAI’s pedagogical potential in reproduc-
ing cultural and historical elements in artistic works (Taama, 2025), teaching traditional
art forms such as Chinese painting (Liu et al., 2025), facilitating cross-cultural heritage
pattern education (Zhu et al., 2025), and supporting collaborative cultural rejuvenation
projects (Ling et al., 2025b). (4) Teacher preparation and transformative pedagogy address
the professional development dimension, examining how educators can be prepared for
AI-integrated art education. This includes studies on GenAI integration in microteaching
lesson study for pre-service visual art teachers (Konakbayeva et al., 2025) and broader
transformative pedagogic reflections on AI integration across multiple educational levels
(Heaton et al., 2024).

• Creative production and visual content generation;
GenAI tools in creative production and visual content generation primarily support
students during the creative production and making stages of art education, functioning as
co-creation partners in the artistic process. Students engage with text-to-image generation
models to transform conceptual ideas into visual outputs, requiring them to develop
proficiency in prompt engineering (Ansone et al., 2025; Heaton et al., 2024; Hutson &
Cotroneo, 2023a, 2023b; Ling et al., 2025b). This iterative process typically involves multiple
refinement cycles (Chiu & Hwang, 2025; Kicklighter et al., 2024; Ling et al., 2025a; Lee et al.,
2024; Zhu et al., 2025), with students adjusting their textual descriptions through three to six
iterations to achieve desired aesthetic outcomes (Ansone et al., 2025). Applications in this
category address fundamental artistic principles including compositional balance, contrast,
unity, and emphasis, while also supporting advanced design considerations such as color
palette harmonization and conceptual development (Ansone et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2025a;
Xu, 2025). GenAI serves as a cultural bridge through custom diffusion model fine-tuning,

enabling students to integrate traditional cultural elements into contemporary digital art

tool through LoRA training techniques, enabling students to explore and apply specialized
aesthetic frameworks from traditional ceramic styles (Kang et al., 2025) to cross-cultural

This pedagogical scaffolding and learning support focus on GenAI’s role as an in-
telligent tutor and learning companion throughout various stages of the artistic learning
process, from initial concept development through skill acquisition and knowledge con-
struction (Kang et al., 2025), positioning GenAI not merely as a tool. Conversational AI
systems provide contextual assistance during creative work sessions, offering hints, clarifi-
cations, and open-ended dialogue about artistic concepts as students encounter challenges

adaptive feedback that adjusts to individual student needs, supporting personalized learn-
ing pathways that account for different skill levels, learning paces, and artistic interests
(Chen et al., 2025b; Hu et al., 2025; Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023b; Liu et al., 2025). During
the conceptual development phase, conversational AI provides pedagogical support by
guiding students through idea generation, helping them understand compositional princi-
ples, refining plot structures for narrative artworks, and evaluating color harmonization
possibilities (Heaton et al., 2024; Xu, 2025; Ling et al., 2025a). Lee et al. (2024) illustrates
these potentials by integrating Stable Diffusion into art-focused STEAM classes, showing
that GenAI not only facilitated creative image generation but also enhanced divergent and
convergent thinking, language expression, and student motivation. Some implementations
employ structured pedagogical frameworks, such as the A/R/Tography approach inte-
grating artist, researcher, and teacher roles (Zhu et al., 2025), QIVE model, which guides
students through questioning, interaction, verification, and examination phases while
integrating GenAI assistance at each stage (Hu et al., 2025); the CIDI (Clarify, Ideate, De-

& Hwang, 2025); the four-module framework (knowledge input, content creation, collabo-
rative creation, and evaluation/feedback), which also reflects this trend by emphasizing
a systematic instructional design that enables GenAI to play a role throughout the entire
learning process (Ling et al., 2025b). Additionally, comparative learning across multiple
GenAI platforms helps students understand different AI tools’ strengths, limitations, and
use cases (Taama, 2025; Kicklighter et al., 2024). Typically implemented in introductory
courses, this approach develops AI literacy by requiring students to critically evaluate
GenAI outputs and make informed tool selections for specific artistic purposes (Heaton
et al., 2024).

3.4. Impact Findings and Practical Recommendations
3.4.1. Impact Findings
The included studies reported predominantly positive findings regarding impacts of
structured GenAI integration on student learning, creativity, and engagement. Students
who received GenAI-supported instruction achieved higher post-test scores and showed
stronger understanding of artistic styles, color composition, and cultural symbolism (Chen
et al., 2025a, 2025b; Chiu & Hwang, 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2025a; Liu et al.,
2025). It also helped reduce cognitive load and improve information processing, with
conversational learning designs proving especially effective (Hu et al., 2025; Kang et al.,
2025). Students became more creative, producing more design drafts, exploring diverse
artistic styles, and working more efficiently during idea development (Chiu & Hwang,
2025; Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023a; Kicklighter et al., 2024; Xu, 2025). They also developed
greater motivation, engagement, and confidence, especially among beginners who had
previously hesitated to create (Chen et al., 2025b; Hu et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025). Over
time, students began to view GenAI as a genuine creative partner rather than a tool for
organizing ideas (Heaton et al., 2024; Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023b). The integration of GenAI
also strengthened cultural understanding, helping students appreciate traditional practices
and think more critically about cultural representation (Liu et al., 2025; Taama, 2025; Zhu
et al., 2025). For teachers, it improved lesson quality, feedback detail, and interdisciplinary
communication (Kang et al., 2025; Konakbayeva et al., 2025). However, challenges persisted,
such as frustration with mismatched AI outputs, over-reliance on automation, uneven tool
accessibility, and temporary declines in teacher self-efficacy (Ansone et al., 2025; Lee et al.,
2024; Ling et al., 2025a).
The pedagogical impact included improved lesson plan quality, enhanced art-related
discourse facilitation, and substantially more detailed instructor feedback (Kang et al., 2025;
Konakbayeva et al., 2025). GenAI supported enhanced design communication, increased
technical vocabulary use, and created opportunities for interdisciplinary learning experi-
ences (Heaton et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2025). However, students experienced challenges.
Some reported frustration when GenAI outputs misaligned with artistic vision, while others
expressed concerns about over-reliance on AI-generated solutions (Ansone et al., 2025; Ling
et al., 2025a). Some students preferred AI-mediated interaction over peer collaboration
(Ling et al., 2025a), while ease-of-use and language proficiency barriers that suggested
varying effectiveness across learners, with some students facing obstacles that others did
not encounter (Ansone et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024). As the instructor level, teaching self-
efficacy among pre-service educators declined uniformly across conditions, suggesting
that integrating new technologies may temporarily challenge instructor confidence even as
pedagogical outcomes improve (Konakbayeva et al., 2025).
3.4.2. Practical Recommendations
• Scaffolding and structured guidance;
Studies recommend using GenAI as a scaffolding tool that supports idea generation
and creative work while keeping students’ critical thinking and creative control (Ansone
et al., 2025; Heaton et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2025; Taama, 2025). Teachers are encouraged to
combine GenAI-assisted activities with manual art projects under supervision to balance
technology and human creativity. This approach can connect traditional art practices
with GenAI through inquiry-based learning that values personal expression and reflective
dialogue (Heaton et al., 2024). Students should be trained to master both traditional artistic
skills and understanding how to work with AI tools (Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023b; Kang
et al., 2025).
• Pedagogical design and learning objectives;

Studies emphasized that effective GenAI integration requires clear instructional frame-
works and redesigned curricula. Recommended models include the QIVE framework,
graded GenAI integration, flipped classrooms with conversational AI instructors, STEAM-
based learning, and mind mapping linked to GenAI-supported storytelling. These models
aim to improve personalization, interaction, knowledge retention, and critical thinking
(Chen et al., 2025a, 2025b; Chiu & Hwang, 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024; Ling et al.,
2025a). These models typically share common features: progressive interaction stages, AI
reflection logs, and three-step workflows involving AI generation, human revision, and
intelligent review. Additionally, pedagogical innovations should include consequence-free
experimentation spaces, collaborative assignments where students interpret and build
upon AI-generated imagery, and continuous curriculum evaluation to stay current with
GenAI advancements (Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023a; Kicklighter et al., 2024).
• Assessment and evaluation methods;
Studies recommended evaluation systems that balance AI and human roles while
ensuring that teachers first guide students in building basic art knowledge and competency-
based learning goals before using GenAI (Hu et al., 2025; Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023a; Ling
et al., 2025a; Xu, 2025). Assessment frameworks must evolve to accommodate GenAI-
augmented learning by incorporating multidimensional indicators that measure creativity,
problem-solving ability, artistic thinking development, cultural accuracy, design quality,
and marketability beyond traditional metrics (Kang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Taama,
2025; Zeng et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025).
• Ethical frameworks and transparent policies;
Ethical use and transparency are central to responsible GenAI integration. Studies
highlight the need for clear policies on how human and AI contributions are reported, for
open communication about boundaries of AI use, and for guidelines addressing copyright,
cultural sensitivity, and job-related concerns (Ansone et al., 2025; Hutson & Cotroneo,
2023a; Kicklighter et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2024; Taama, 2025; Xu, 2025). Particularly in
culturally themed projects, researchers suggest improving GenAI’s cultural awareness
through diverse training data and cross-cultural knowledge systems. They recommend
co-creation models that combine automation with artistic interpretation to preserve cultural
meaning and authenticity (Zhu et al., 2025).
• Technical improvements and system optimization;
Technical improvements include optimizing GenAI tools for accurate semantic match-
ing and color semantic matching, expanding dataset diversity, incorporating ethical risk
monitoring mechanisms, and strengthening teacher training and technical support (Kon-
akbayeva et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025).
• Professional development and institutional support.
Institutional backing is vital for sustainable GenAI integration. Schools and universi-
ties should develop policies, training programs, and workshops that promote thoughtful
GenAI use through continuous planning, teaching, observation, and feedback. They should
also form partnerships and update digital strategies that teach prompt engineering and AI
literacy as part of structured art education (Ansone et al., 2025; Konakbayeva et al., 2025;
Ling et al., 2025b; Xu, 2025).

4.1. Implications for Art Education Practice
This section examines four key implications of GenAI integration in art education practice.

4.1.1. GenAI as Process Tool: Expanding Rather than Replacing Artistic Literacy
A key feature of GenAI use in art education is its strong focus on text-to-image
generation, which differs from the text-based applications that dominate most GenAI
research in general education (Heilala et al., 2025). This distinction reflects art education’s
fundamental concerns with visual thinking and creative production. In included studies,
GenAI is treated mainly as a process tool rather than a final product maker. Students
often combine several tools—traditional materials like ceramics, painting, and sketching;
digital software such as Procreate, Photoshop, and Adobe Color; and GenAI platforms
like DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, or customized systems—to refine and expand their creative
work (Heaton et al., 2024; Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023b; Kang et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2025a;
Zeng et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Available findings suggest that GenAI can enhance
creativity, idea generation, and engagement (Chiu & Hwang, 2025; Kicklighter et al., 2024;
Xu, 2025). It does not replace traditional skill development but supports it, extending what
it means to be artistically literate (Andrae, 2025). This reflects long-standing views in art
education that digital and traditional literacies should work together, not compete (Snyder
& Bulfin, 2007). The rise in prompt engineering as a new creative skill (Hutson & Cotroneo,
2023a; Oppenlaender et al., 2025) continues art education’s long history of growth through
technology. Just as photography and digital art once broadened creative practice, GenAI
now adds another layer to how students learn, create, and express themselves (Eri¸ sti &
Freedman, 2024; Choi & Piro, 2009; Paul, 2023).
However, describing GenAI as a “process tool” needs careful clarification. The re-
viewed studies do not all show that students consistently refine GenAI outputs through
meaningful traditional creative work. The extent of integration often depends on several
conditions: whether the course design explicitly includes both manual and GenAI-based
stages, whether instructors understand both traditional and AI-supported methods, and
whether institutions provide support for multi-step creative workflows.
Different studies treated GenAI in different ways. Some required students to manu-
ally refine AI-generated outputs (Hutson & Cotroneo, 2023b; Kang et al., 2025). In these
implementations, GenAI functioned as a starting point or reference within a larger man-
ual creative process, positioning students as primary creative agents making intentional
choices about how to transform AI-generated content. Others built GenAI into structured
learning cycles where refinement happened naturally through repeated iterations (Chiu &
Hwang, 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2025a). In these cases, GenAI supported iterative
exploration rather than providing final products, creating process engagement.
A smaller number of studies positioned GenAI as a constant feedback system that
supported students throughout their creative process (Chen et al., 2025b; Zeng et al., 2025).
In these instances, the boundary between GenAI contribution and student contribution
became blurred. Studies that documented the integration of GenAI within expanded artistic
literacy (Liu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2025a) typically involved instructors
with pedagogical intentionality and curriculum redesign, suggesting that the “process
tool” characterization applies primarily to carefully designed implementations rather than
representing an inherent feature of GenAI tools themselves. Conversely, studies lacking
explicit pedagogical frameworks or instructor guidance (which were excluded from this
review) likely result in students treating GenAI as a shortcut rather than a process partner.
4.1.2. Pedagogical Design and Learning Outcomes
The use of mixed methods and structured pedagogical frameworks, such as the
QIVE model (Hu et al., 2025), scaffolded instruction (Liu et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025),
and mind mapping with GenAI (Chiu & Hwang, 2025), suggests that effective GenAI
integration depends far more on intentional instructional design rather than casual tool
https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci16010047

=== PDF PAGE 21 ===
Educ. Sci. 2026, 16, 47 21 of 33
adoption. To understand why design matters, it is important to look at how GenAI produces
learning gains and under what conditions those gains occur. The improvements in learning
performance found in several studies (Chen et al., 2025a, 2025b; Ling et al., 2025a) suggest
that GenAI enhances learning through two main pathways.
First, GenAI provides cognitive scaffolding by reducing unnecessary mental load
during idea generation. Students face fewer technical obstacles when developing ideas be-
cause GenAI can quickly create visual samples, allowing them to focus on artistic judgment
and concept refinement instead of manual execution (Hu et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2025).
This process is especially clear in courses where GenAI is embedded within structured
creative stages—ideation, visualization, and refinement—rather than used for immedi-
ate final production (Ling et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Second, GenAI
promotes personalized learning through adaptive feedback. Conversational AI can offer
real-time suggestions matched to a learner’s skill level and interests (Chen et al., 2025b;
Hu et al., 2025). This aligns with social cognitive theory, which holds that motivation and
self-efficacy increase when learners receive appropriate challenges and targeted support
(B. J. Zimmerman & Schunk, 2012).
However, keeping these benefits outside controlled research settings is difficult. Their
success depends on teacher expertise in designing adaptive activities and on students’
willingness to engage with AI feedback. These conditions are not always present in
real classrooms. Konakbayeva et al. (2025) found that pre-service teachers’ self-efficacy
declined after AI-supported microteaching interventions, even though lesson quality and
teaching competence improved. This suggests that while GenAI-enhanced approaches can
strengthen instructional outcomes, they may simultaneously heighten teachers’ awareness
of the complexities of teaching, leading to reduced confidence. Similar challenges have long
existed in digital art education, where teachers report stress, heavier workloads, and limited
training when new technologies are introduced (Black & Browning, 2011). This indicates
that professional development must build not only technical skill but also pedagogical
resilience to handle ongoing technological change (Fernández-Batanero et al., 2021).
The learning theories behind GenAI use reveal that constructivist and cognitive per-
spectives dominate among studies with explicit frameworks. Constructivist approaches
emphasized active exploration and iterative problem-solving (Lester et al., 1999), par-
ticularly suited to how GenAI tools support experimental ideation. Cognitive theories
emphasized load reduction and scaffolding (Paas et al., 2003), aligning with GenAI‘s ca-
pacity to support specific cognitive processes. Yet, more than one-third of studies lacked a
stated theoretical foundation, showing that GenAI-related art education research is still
exploratory. Many implementations appear driven by innovation rather than theory, echo-
ing Selwyn’s (2010) view that educational technology often favors novelty over grounded
inquiry. Some studies that reported learning gains, such as those by Kicklighter et al. (2024)
and Xu (2025), did not specify any learning theory, leaving the causes of improvement
unclear. Future research should move beyond asking whether GenAI improves learning
and instead focus on how and why these gains occur under different teaching conditions.
Finally, assessment practices in this field remain limited. Although some studies
proposed multidimensional evaluation frameworks (Kang et al., 2025; Taama, 2025; Zeng
et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025), most still used post-tests or self-reports. These tools may not
fully capture art-specific learning dimensions such as aesthetic growth or maintaining a
personal vision while using generative tools. As a result, higher test scores after GenAI use
may not truly reflect deeper artistic understanding or creative growth—they might only
show better prompt-writing ability. This gap in measurement is critical for art educators,
who must determine whether GenAI integration truly advances artistic learning or simply
meets performance metrics without enriching the creative process.

4.1.3. Cultural Heritage Education: Opportunities and Critical Considerations
The application of GenAI in cultural heritage education (Liu et al., 2025; Ling et al.,
2025b; Taama, 2025; Zhu et al., 2025) improves cultural understanding and cross-cultural
competencies when implemented with deliberate pedagogical intentionality. Students
engaged with these applications by translating their tacit cultural knowledge into explicit
descriptions for GenAI systems, then iteratively refining outputs through critical evaluation
to ensure cultural accuracy and authenticity. These improvements emerge specifically
when GenAI is positioned as subordinate to students’ cultural knowledge, when critical
evaluation of outputs is required, and when educator authority over cultural authenticity
is maintained. Custom diffusion model fine-tuning for traditional art forms (Liu et al.,
2025) exemplifies one such intentional approach, demonstrates technical possibilities for
culturally responsive GenAI applications, addressing concerns about Western bias in
mainstream generative models (Ghosh et al., 2024). However, the recommendation to
enhance GenAI’s cultural intelligence through cross-cultural corpus training (Zhu et al.,
2025) is significant because it comes from researchers who themselves employed technical
approaches including LoRA training and custom model fine-tuning. Even with such
specialized interventions, they acknowledge persistent limitations in current systems’
capacity to authentically represent diverse cultural contexts. This concern extends to
technical accuracy to encompass issues of cultural appropriation, authenticity, and the
power dynamics embedded in dataset creation and model training (Ferrara, 2024; Foka
& Griffin, 2024), raising the question of whose cultural perspectives GenAI privileges.
Art educators working with GenAI in cultural contexts must therefore cultivate critical
AI literacy that interrogates not only outputs but also the underlying data structures and
algorithmic processes that shape cultural representation (Spennemann, 2024). The challenge
is that deep data interrogation and the customization necessary to address bias necessitate
technical expertise beyond most educators’ reach. The implication is troubling: positive
cultural heritage outcomes in this review appear contingent on additional technical work
that most educators cannot perform, suggesting that GenAI’s cultural heritage potential
may be accessible primarily to well-resourced institutions with machine learning expertise.
4.1.4. Equity and Access Challenges
Although few studies directly examined equity, the strong concentration of research
in East Asia and higher education raises important concerns about fairness and generaliz-
ability. The dominance of higher education likely reflects differences in resources because
universities often have greater access to technology, funding, and time for designing struc-
tured implementations. In contrast, K-12 schools typically face more limits in infrastructure,
technical support, and teacher training related to technology use and classroom manage-
ment (Hew & Brush, 2007; Ross, 2020). This gap highlights a clear imbalance between the
well-equipped environments studied and the realities of many schools. For example, one
K-12 study (Zeng et al., 2025) used a carefully designed mobile platform called MAI-SGD
that required custom development, showing that positive results from such controlled
settings may not easily apply to resource-limited classrooms without additional investment.
Another issue is the lack of research from the Global South beyond East and Southeast Asia.
This absence leaves open questions about how GenAI might work in different educational
systems, cultural contexts, and levels of technological access. Language accessibility adds a
further layer of inequality. While conversational tools like ChatGPT can handle multiple
languages (Lai et al., 2023), many text-to-image generators rely heavily on English-language
inputs (Reviriego & Merino-Gómez, 2022). Lee et al. (2024) reported that Korean elementary
students had to use Google Translator to turn their ideas into English before submitting
prompts to DreamStudio, which performed better with English text. This dependency

matters because translating creative ideas can distort meaning, reduce expressiveness, and
make spontaneous artistic communication harder. It also reinforces cultural bias because
students from non-English-speaking communities must adapt to tools trained primarily on
English-language and Western artistic data, which may underrepresent their own cultural
traditions and creative voices. Current evidence captures only the earliest implementations
under privileged conditions. As research expands in 2026 and beyond—ideally encom-
passing under-resourced schools, diverse cultural contexts, and K-12 settings—the field
will gain clearer understanding of whether GenAI’s observed benefits generalize across
educational contexts or require substantial revision based on resource availability, cultural
fit, and institutional support.
4.2. Limitations
Several limitations restrict the scope and generalizability of this review’s findings.
These limitations fall into three categories: research maturity and evidence depth, general-
izability and contextual specificity, and incomplete reporting and methodological precision.
4.2.1. Temporal Limitations and Absence of Long-Term Evidence
The temporal concentration of 19 studies entirely within 2023–2025 with the majority
published in 2025 indicates that empirical research on GenAI in art education is extremely
recent. This recency creates several limitations. This concentration raises questions about
whether observed patterns will remain stable as the field matures and more diverse imple-
mentations are studied. Moreover, the field’s novelty means that long-term effects cannot
yet be documented. GenAI tools suitable for art education have only been widely available
since 2023, making multi-year longitudinal studies methodologically impossible at this
stage regardless of researcher intentions. First, all findings describe short-term outcomes
documented in single courses or semesters; no studies follow students across multiple
years to examine sustained impacts on artistic development, skill progression, or long-term
creative identity. We cannot determine whether initial improvements persist after students’
novelty reactions to GenAI fade or whether sustained engagement with GenAI affects
students‘ long-term artistic practice. Second, the rapid evolution of GenAI tools makes
many findings potentially time-bound. Studies conducted with 2024 iterations of tools
may provide limited guidance for practice using significantly improved tools available in
2026 or beyond. Third, the concentrations of publications in a single year (2025) suggests
this research may be riding a peak of institutional attention and funding; longer-term
sustainability of research and implementation remains unknown.
4.2.2. Generalizability and Contextual Specificity
Several factors constrain how broadly these findings can be applied. Across all in-
cluded studies, the total number of participants is about 1211, spread across a range of
educational settings and implementation models. Across all included studies, the total
number of participants is about 1211. Although this provides a reasonable foundation
for an emerging field, the distribution across diverse contexts and relatively small indi-
vidual study sample sizes constrain broader generalization. Because this review focused
specifically on studies involving direct teaching and learning with educators or students,
we necessarily excluded non-pedagogical perspectives and studies involving non-student
populations such as museum visitors or hobbyists. While this focus strengthens the peda-
gogical relevance of our findings, it also means our review does not capture insights from
studies of GenAI in art education by non-student populations. Moreover, most studies were
conducted in Asian and higher education contexts, which limits understanding of how
GenAI might work in K-12 classrooms or under-resourced regions. Educational practices,
creative pedagogies, and technology adoption patterns vary significantly across cultural

contexts, suggesting that findings from predominantly Asian and higher education imple-
mentations may not directly transfer to other settings. Beyond these geographic constraints,
the overlap between art and design education created classification challenges. Some
studies addressed topics like design history or color composition, which belong to both art
and design curricula. These were included when authors framed them as art education
or cross-disciplinary in nature. Future reviews could strengthen definitional clarity by
distinguishing between art-focused and design-focused studies. Together, these factors
mean that the reported results reflect a relatively specific population within particular
cultural and institutional contexts.
4.2.3. Incomplete Reporting and Methodological Gaps
Some studies provided incomplete details about participants or GenAI tools. For
instance, Heaton et al. (2024) described implementations across three education levels
but only reported participant counts for secondary ( n = 6) and graduate ( n = 14) levels.
Chiu and Hwang (2025) and Ling et al. (2025b) mentioned using text-to-image generation
systems but did not specify which tools were employed. Although these omissions limit
the precision of our characterization, these studies were still included because they demon-
strated clear teaching design, sufficient analytic detail, and meaningful contributions to the
review’s guiding questions. Additionally, few studies reported in detail the precise GenAI
implementation duration, frequency of GenAI use, or fidelity of the pedagogical design
across classrooms: information needed to understand implementation intensity. These
reporting gaps mean that readers cannot fully assess whether observed outcomes reflect
GenAI itself or confounding implementation factors (teacher quality, class size, grading
structure, motivation effects, etc.). Beyond individual study reporting gaps, the geographic
distribution of research created methodological barriers to regional comparison. First, some
studies involved multi-institutional collaborations spanning different countries; to avoid
duplicate counting, we used the first author’s first institutional affiliation for categorization
(as reported in Section 3.1.2), but this approach introduces potential inconsistency. Second,
institutional location did not always correspond to implementation location. Third, studies
were distributed across different educational levels (K-12 and higher education), diverse
GenAI tools, and varied pedagogical approaches, creating a small and heterogeneous sub-
sample unsuitable for comparison. These methodological constraints meant we could not
empirically determine whether observed outcomes differ systematically across geographic
regions or national contexts. Consequently, while we can report that findings dispropor-
tionately reflect Asian and higher education contexts (given the concentration of studies in
both dimensions), the extent and nature of potential regional bias remains unclear.
4.3. Future Research Directions
Future research priorities include evaluation frameworks, longitudinal studies with
larger samples and more complete reporting of technical specifications and participant
characteristics to enable meta-analytic synthesis and effect size calculations, cross-cultural
applicability investigations, and interdisciplinary applications beyond traditional art educa-
tion contexts (Chen et al., 2025b; Kang et al., 2025; Konakbayeva et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025;
Zhu et al., 2025). Research should also examine differential effectiveness across learner char-
acteristics, including prior artistic skill levels, cognitive styles, cultural backgrounds, and
learning preferences. Related to this, understanding for whom and under what conditions
GenAI enhances learning would enable more precise, equitable implementation. In parallel,
studies investigating optimal sequencing of traditional and GenAI-augmented instruction
could identify junctures where GenAI introduction maximally benefits rather than disrupts
skill development trajectories. Beyond these considerations, future research should also

expand contextual diversity. This includes both resource-diverse settings (rural schools
and low-income communities to address equity concerns and identify low-infrastructure
implementation strategies) and format-diverse contexts (informal learning environments
such as community art programs, museums, and online platforms to extend understanding
beyond formal education).
5. Conclusions
This systematic review synthesized 19 empirical studies on GenAI use in art education
published between January 2019 and August 2025. The results reveal a rapidly developing
field with encouraging early outcomes in learning, creativity, and cultural understanding—
particularly in well-designed and well-supported settings. However, given the small
sample size, geographic concentration in Asian contexts, and the recency of most studies,
these findings represent preliminary patterns rather than established trends. Important gaps
remain regarding long-term effects, effective pedagogical models across diverse contexts,
equity issues in under-resourced settings, and ethical challenges of GenAI integration.
The central conclusion is not simply that GenAI improves art education. Rather, GenAI
can enhance art education only when used under specific conditions: with intentional
pedagogical design, adequate teacher training, institutional support, clear learning goals,
and integration alongside traditional art-making methods. These conditions are far from
universal. In many schools and regions with limited funding, infrastructure, or English-
language resources, the positive results reported in these studies may not appear without
major investment.
This raises several pressing questions for the field. Will educational systems commit
the resources needed to implement GenAI thoughtfully and equitably? Or will GenAI
spread through convenience-driven adoption where technology dictates teaching methods
instead of supporting clear pedagogical goals? Will its benefits remain concentrated in
resource-rich, English-speaking higher education, widening existing inequalities? And how
can educators build the resilience and theoretical grounding to use GenAI responsibly while
continuing to develop imagination, perseverance, and aesthetic judgment—the enduring
foundations of art education?
Future research must move beyond proving that GenAI can enhance art learning.
It must determine how it does so, under what conditions, for whom, and with what
consequences for art education’s deeper aims. The answers will decide whether GenAI
becomes a true partner in nurturing human creativity or another short-lived innovation that
widens educational divides and diverts attention from essential, human-centered learning.
As this systematic review captures research conducted during an exceptionally dy-
namic period in both GenAI technology development and educational implementation,
readers should interpret these findings as representing early-stage patterns rather than
definitive conclusions. The field’s rapid evolution suggests that follow-up systematic
reviews conducted in subsequent years will likely reveal expanded empirical evidence,
potentially confirming, refining, or revising the preliminary patterns identified here.
